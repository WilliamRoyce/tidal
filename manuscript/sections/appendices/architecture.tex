\section{\TIDAL{} software architecture}%
\label{Architecture}

\paragraph*{Overview}
This appendix sets out the architecture of
\emph{Tensor Integration and Derivation for Any Lagrangian} (\TIDAL{}),
the computer-algebra and numerical software with which we obtained the
results of \cref{Results}.
The symbolic stage, written in \Mathematica{}~\cite{mathematica} on the
\xAct{}~\cite{martingarcia2007xact} suite, expands a user-supplied
Lagrangian to quadratic order around a chosen background and exports
the resulting linear field equations as a portable specification; the
numerical stage, written in \Python{}, reads the specification and
integrates the equations in time.
All equations of motion are derived automatically from the Lagrangian;
the user specifies only the theory.
The architecture is shown in~\cref{ArchitectureDiagram}.

\paragraph*{Symbolic derivation of equations of motion}
The symbolic stage takes the user-supplied Lagrangian, expands it to
quadratic order around the declared background using
\xPert{}~\cite{brizuela2009xpert}, applies the Euler–Lagrange variation
in \xAct{}, and decomposes the resulting tensor equations into scalar
components with the aid of \xTras{}~\cite{nutma2014xtras}.
Gauge fixing is carried out either by adding a gauge-fixing term to the
Lagrangian or by imposing the gauge condition as a constraint after the
variation; the specific gauge is declared in the theory specification
and is not restricted to a fixed set of choices.
Backgrounds may be flat or diagonal-metric, and constant,
position-dependent, or time-dependent.
The symbolic operations, including the handling of the
higher-derivative $\tilde{R}^2$ terms that distinguish PGT from
Einstein gravity, are described in \cref{SymbolicComputation}.

\paragraph*{Equation specification}
The equation specification is the sole interface between the symbolic
and numerical stages.
It records each field's equation of motion as a sum of differential
operators with parameter-dependent coefficients, the Hamiltonian
density in the same form, and a declaration of each field's time
order — wave-like, first-order, or algebraically constrained.
The specification is written in JSON so that it can be read by the
numerical stage with no dependence on the symbolic toolchain;
decoupling the two stages in this way allows the same symbolic
derivation to support arbitrary grid resolutions, initial conditions,
and parameter choices without re-derivation, which is what makes
parameter surveys of the kind reported in \cref{Results} feasible.

\paragraph*{Numerical integration}
Numerical evolution is carried out by one of several backends,
selected automatically from the algebraic structure of the equations.
For the surveys reported in \cref{Results}, the Fourier modal solver
is used: it integrates the equations to machine precision via matrix
exponentiation, as detailed in \cref{ModalSolver}.
When the modal conditions are not met, \TIDAL{} falls back to
SUNDIALS~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials} \textsc{IDA} for systems with
algebraic constraints, SUNDIALS \textsc{CVODE} for adaptive ODE
integration, a leapfrog integrator when a Hamiltonian is available, or
\SciPy{}~\cite{virtanen2020scipy} \texttt{solve\_ivp} as a
general-purpose fallback.
The dispatch logic and the eigendecomposition algorithm are described
in \cref{NumericalEvolution}.

\paragraph*{Observables}
The numerical evolution produces field snapshots at each saved
timestep, from which physics observables are extracted.
For the surveys of \cref{Results}, the observable is the
graviton–photon conversion probability $\Pconv(t)$; the run-maximum
$\Pmax$ provides the quantity compared between theories in the
sector-by-sector study of \cref{SectorSurvey}.
