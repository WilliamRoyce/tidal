\section{\TIDAL{} pipeline architecture}%
\label{Architecture}

\paragraph*{Overview}
We document here the architecture of \TIDAL{} (Tensor Integration and
Derivation for Any Lagrangian), a two-stage symbolic–numerical pipeline
that automates the derivation and solution of linearised equations of motion
for any quadratic field theory expressed as a Lagrangian.
The symbolic stage, implemented in \Mathematica{}~\cite{mathematica} via the
\xAct{}~\cite{martingarcia2007xact} tensor-algebra suite, ingests a TOML
theory specification and exports a self-contained JSON equation specification;
the numerical stage, implemented in \Python{}, reads this specification and
evolves the field equations in time, returning physics observables.
All equations of motion are derived automatically from the Lagrangian; the
user specifies only the theory, not the equations.
The pipeline operates exclusively in the linearised regime: quadratic
Lagrangians producing linear PDEs; nonlinear extensions are outside the scope
of the present implementation.
The pipeline is shown in~\cref{ArchitectureDiagram}; the following named
paragraphs expand each stage.

\paragraph*{Symbolic derivation of equations of motion}
The symbolic stage derives the linearised equations of motion directly
from the Lagrangian, eliminating the need for manual symbolic algebra.
For the Poincaré gauge theory Lagrangians surveyed in \cref{Results},
the spin-connection field carries 24 independent components, and
expanding the Euler–Lagrange equations by hand is infeasible across the
theory classes surveyed; \xAct{}, \xPert{}~\cite{brizuela2009xpert}, and
\xTras{}~\cite{nutma2014xtras} automate this step for arbitrary quadratic
Lagrangians in any coordinate basis.
The user provides a TOML Lagrangian specification naming the field
content, background, and gauge conditions; the derivation is then
carried out automatically, without further user intervention.
\xPert{} linearises the Lagrangian about the declared background (flat
metric, uniform $\Bzero$) to quadratic order; \xAct{} applies the
Euler–Lagrange variation and decomposes the tensor equations to their
scalar components; gauge conditions are imposed algebraically using
presets declared in the specification (Lorenz, de~Donder, Coulomb,
temporal, or axial).
The symbolic operations — including the Euler–Lagrange velocity form,
PGT component decomposition, and handling of higher-derivative
$\tilde{R}^2$ terms — are described in detail in
\cref{SymbolicComputation}.

\paragraph*{JSON equation specification}
Without a language-agnostic intermediate representation, any change to a
solver parameter, grid resolution, or initial condition would require
re-running the Wolfram symbolic stage.
The JSON specification solves this by decoupling the two pipeline stages
completely: the symbolic stage exports a self-contained JSON file that is
the sole interface between the stages, and the numerical solver reads it
with no knowledge of \xAct{}, the derivation history, or the symbolic
representation.
The specification records each field's equation of motion as a sum of
differential operators with parameter-dependent coefficients; the
Hamiltonian density is recorded in the same form.
The specification also declares whether each field is wave-like,
first-order in time, or algebraically constrained, enabling the
numerical stage to select and configure its evolution scheme without
knowledge of the underlying theory.
The same specification supports arbitrary grid resolutions, initial
conditions, and parameter choices without re-derivation.

\paragraph*{Numerical backend auto-selection}
\TIDAL{} examines the equation specification and dispatches automatically
to the appropriate solver backend, without requiring the user to specify
a numerical scheme.
The appropriate backend depends on the algebraic structure of the
specification: applying a time-stepping scheme to a constant-coefficient
system amenable to exact eigendecomposition discards machine precision
unnecessarily; applying a symplectic integrator to a system with
algebraic constraints fails to enforce those constraints.
\TIDAL{} determines the appropriate backend automatically, preventing
both failure modes.
The highest-priority backend, the Fourier modal solver, is selected when
the background metric is flat, all boundary conditions are periodic, all
coefficients are time-independent, and all differential operators belong
to the supported set; this backend delivers machine-precision solutions
via matrix exponentiation~(\cref{ModalSolver}) and was used for all
simulation campaigns reported in \cref{Results}.
When the modal conditions are not met, \TIDAL{} falls back in priority
order to: \textsc{IDA} (SUNDIALS~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials} DAE
solver, for systems with algebraically constrained fields);
\textsc{CVODE} (SUNDIALS adaptive BDF, the default wave-equation
backend); leapfrog (symplectic integration when a Hamiltonian is
available); and \SciPy{}~\cite{virtanen2020scipy} \texttt{solve\_ivp}
(general-purpose fallback).
The full backend-selection logic is shown in~\cref{SolverFlowchart} and
the eigendecomposition algorithm is detailed in \cref{NumericalEvolution}.

\paragraph*{Simulation output and observables}
From a completed simulation, \TIDAL{} computes the graviton–photon
conversion probability and its maximum over the run, field energy
densities, spectral decompositions, and mixing coefficients; ancillary
diagnostics include effective masses and signal asymptotes.
The simulation data are preserved as a checkpoint directory of field
snapshots, together with parameter metadata and the originating
equation specification.
For the surveys of \cref{Results}, the primary observable is the
conversion probability $\Pconv(t)$, with $\Pmax$ extracted for the
sector-by-sector comparisons of \cref{SectorSurvey}.
Parameter sweeps parallelise the simulate–measure pipeline across a
user-specified parameter grid; the systematic survey of the PGT
coupling space reported in \cref{Results} was made possible by this
automation, at a scale not accessible by manual simulation.
