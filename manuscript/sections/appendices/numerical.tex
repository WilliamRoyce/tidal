\section{Numerical evolution of the linearised system}%
\label{NumericalEvolution}

The numerical stage of \TIDAL{} reads the
equation specification produced by~\cref{SymbolicComputation} and
evolves the linearised fields on a Cartesian sampling grid,
supporting both flat and curved spacetime metrics. The grid and
field-container design draws on the \pypde{}
framework~\cite{zwicker2020pypde}. \TIDAL{} provides common
infrastructure and a selector that chooses
among several solvers from the algebraic structure of the
equation system. \Cref{Results} uses the Fourier modal solver
of~\cref{ModalSolver}.

\paragraph*{Solver selection hierarchy} The solver family is
heterogeneous because the equation systems \TIDAL{} handles span
differential--algebraic (DAE), pure-wave, and spatially-varying-background
regimes. DAEs from gauge sectors require simultaneous
algebraic-and-differential solution~\cite{henneaux1992quantization};
pure wave systems admit explicit symplectic integration; and
time-independent systems on periodic boundaries are eligible for the
spectral modal solver. The
selector~(\cref{SolverDispatchFlowchart}) chooses the first applicable
solver from the ordered rules:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Modal} when the metric is flat ($\sqrt{|g|}$ is unity), the spatial boundaries are
    all periodic, and the coefficients are time-independent;
    algebraic constraints, when present, can be eliminated per-mode by
    Schur complement (\cref{ModalSolver}).
  \item \textbf{IDA} (Implicit Differential-Algebraic solver,
    \SUNDIALS{}~\cite{gardner2022sundials,hindmarsh2005sundials}) when
    any of the following holds: the system
    carries algebraic constraints; the
    system carries first-order equations; or any right-hand side carries a
    $\PD{_t}$ operator acting on another field (a dissipative
    cross-coupling).
  \item \textbf{CVODE} (\SUNDIALS{} adaptive ODE,
    BDF~\cite{gardner2022sundials,hindmarsh2005sundials}) when the
    system is a pure second-order wave.
\end{enumerate}
The leapfrog (\cref{OtherBackends}) and \SciPy{} solvers are opt-in only.

\subsection{Equation substrate and state layout}%
\label{EquationSubstrate}

\paragraph*{State representation} The \TIDAL{} state vector packs each
field's slots into a flat array, with the number of slots per field
set by the time-derivative order of its equation of motion:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Wave fields} (\lstinline!time_order!~$=2$) contribute
    two slots: values for the field $\varphi$, and
    its time derivative
    $v_\varphi \equiv \partial_t \varphi$ at the current time. The two arrays
    are linked by
    \begin{align}
      \partial_t \varphi = v_\varphi
      \, ,
      \qquad
      \partial_t v_\varphi = \mathcal{L}[\varphi, v_\varphi]
      \, ,
      \label{ELVelocityForm}
    \end{align}
    which advances the field from the velocity slot.
  \item \textbf{First-order fields} (\lstinline!time_order!~$=1$)
    contribute one slot: values for the field. The
    integrator advances this array using $\partial_t \varphi$ computed from
    the right-hand-side specification.
  \item \textbf{Constraint fields} (\lstinline!time_order!~$=0$)
    contribute one slot: values for the field. The values
    are not advanced by an integrator; they are determined at
    each timestep by solving the field's algebraic equation
    against the
    current values of the other fields.

  An example is the Maxwell scalar
  potential $A_0$: in the Lorenz gauge it
  is determined at each instant by the
  Poisson equation $\nabla^2 A_0 = \mathcal{S}[A_i]$, with the source
  $\mathcal{S}[A_i]$ supplied by the spatial components.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{Equation specification and evaluator}
The equations of motion (EOM) come from the JSON specification (\cref{JSONExample})
produced by~\cref{SymbolicComputation}. At each call the evaluator
parameter-resolves the coefficients, applies each operator using its
implementation, and assembles the solver's callable.

\paragraph*{Velocity form versus canonical phase space}
The kinetic matrix $K_{ij}$ is the Hessian of the linearised
Lagrangian density with respect to the field
velocities~\cite{henneaux1992quantization},
\begin{align}
  K_{ij} \equiv \frac{\partial^2 \lag}{\partial(\partial_t \varphi_i)\,
    \partial(\partial_t \varphi_j)}
  \, ,
  \label{KineticMatrixDef}
\end{align}
evaluated at the background. The canonical momenta are defined by
\begin{align}
  p_i \equiv \frac{\partial \lag}{\partial(\partial_t \varphi_i)}
  \, ,
  \label{CanonicalMomenta}
\end{align}
which gives the linear
relation $p_i = K_{ij}\, v_j$. To express the Hamiltonian density
$\ham = p_i\, v_i - \lag$ in terms of the canonical coordinates $(q, p)$
one must
eliminate the velocities $v_i = (K^{-1})_{ij}\, p_j$.

\TIDAL{} chooses the velocity form for three reasons. (i) It avoids
the symbolic inversion at derivation time. For
non-diagonal $K$ the entries of $K^{-1}$ are rational expressions
whose symbolic size grows factorially with the number of fields.
Keeping the velocity form $(q, v)$ instead passes $K$ directly to the
solver, with no inversion. (ii) The same flat-state representation
accommodates wave, first-order, and constraint fields, so every
solver sees a single residual function $F(t, y, \dot y) = 0$ or
$\dot y = f(t, y)$.
(iii) It is well-defined even when the kinetic matrix is singular.
A singular $K$ signals that the field variables in
the Lagrangian are not all independent and prevents the
change of variables from $(q, v)$ to $(q, p)$; the
canonical formulation must instead be recovered by the
Dirac--Bergmann constraint analysis of~\cref{DiracBergmann}.
% The velocity form sidesteps the change of variables entirely.

\paragraph*{Coefficient evaluator} Each right-hand-side term carries an
\xAct{} symbolic coefficient,
resolved at run time against the parameter assignments and possibly the spatial coordinate grid. The
\lstinline!CoefficientEvaluator! class implements a cache to
amortise these per-call evaluations. The cache (as necessary) stores pre-resolved constants;
parsed parameter-resolved expressions; spatial
grids of position-dependentcoefficient values; and per-timestep
deduplication of time-dependent coefficients.

\subsection{Spatial discretisation}%
\label{SpatialDiscretisation}

\paragraph*{Finite-difference stencils} The default spatial
discretisation is a central-difference scheme on a uniform Cartesian
grid, where the stencil coefficients are those of Fornberg's algorithm~\cite{fornberg1988generation}.
The expected algebraic convergence
rates are realised on a smooth gradient
test in~\cref{FDConvergence}. Boundary conditions
% (periodic, Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin)
are handled uniformly by a ghost-cell
formula~\cite{leveque2007finite}.
The finite-difference (FD) path is the fallback for systems on which
neither the modal solver of~\cref{ModalSolver} nor the pseudo-spectral
path of~\cref{OtherBackends} is eligible.

\subsection{Fourier modal solver}%
\label{ModalSolver}

\paragraph*{Modal evolution} The state $(q, v)$ is transformed
to Fourier space, and the right-hand-side operators are
applied as Fourier symbols. For a
system with constant coefficients, each $k$-mode
decouples, and the evolution reduces to a finite-dimensional linear
ODE on the state vector~$\hat y(k)$ (the column that
stacks the amplitudes of every field
$\hat\varphi^I(k)$ and velocity $\hat v_\varphi^I(k)$)
\begin{align}
  \frac{\diff}{\diff t} \hat y(k) = M(k)\, \hat y(k)
  \, ,
  \quad
  \hat y(k, t) = \exp\!\big(M(k)\, t\big)\, \hat y(k, 0)
  .
  \label{ModalPerMode}
\end{align}

Because $\exp(M(k)\,t_{\rm end})$ reaches the endpoint in a single
application, the wall time is independent of the integration endpoint
$t_{\rm end}$. Each output snapshot is one matrix--vector product per
mode. The decoupling fails
when coefficients are position-dependent, and the
evolution is computed instead by Krylov action described later.

\paragraph*{Pad\'e scaling and squaring} The per-mode matrix
exponential in~\cref{ModalPerMode} is computed via the Pad\'e
scaling-and-squaring method of
\lstinline!scipy.linalg.expm!~\cite{higham2009scaling}. We do not
employ the eigendecomposition $M(k) = V D V^{-1}$ with
$\exp(M t) = V \exp(D\, t) V^{-1}$ because the eigenvector matrix $V$
becomes ill-conditioned in the
Poincar\'e gauge sector: near-degenerate eigenvalues arise from a
variety of sources and the worst-case condition number
$\operatorname{cond}(V)$~\cite{golub2013matrix,higham2008functions} can exceed $10^{13}$ on the
theories of~\cref{Results}. Above this conditioning, diagonalisation
accumulates round-off far beyond the accuracy threshold of
matrix-exponential
algorithms~\cite{higham2008functions,molervanloan2003nineteen,golub2013matrix,trefethen1997numerical};
we adopt the recommendation of Higham~\cite[\S~2.3]{higham2008functions} and
replace it with the Pad\'e path,\footnote{The eigendecomposition path
remains available internally for the closed-form Duhamel kernel used
in the perturbative-reduction scheme of~\cref{PerturbativeReduction},
which requires the per-eigenmode expansion explicitly.}
which is robust at arbitrary
$\operatorname{cond}(V)$~\cite{higham2009scaling,higham2008functions}
% The Pad\'e per-mode wall time is competitive with the former
% eigendecomposition path across the entire regime measured on three
% theories: the Raffelt--Stodolsky
% plasma--graviton~\cite{raffelt1988mixing},
% Einstein--Maxwell~\cite{gertsenshtein1962wave,boccaletti1970conversion}, and
% Fierz--Pauli massive gravity~\cite{fierzpauli1939relativistic,boulwaredeser1972finite,hinterbichler2012theoretical} in $2{+}1$D
% (\cref{fig:PadeBenchmark}).
at no average performance cost (\cref{fig:PadeBenchmark}).

\paragraph*{Block-aware union-find} The evolution matrix $M(k)$
inherits the block structure of the underlying coupled-field
equations. A typical multi-field theory decomposes into several
decoupled sub-blocks % --- for example, the $h_\times\!\leftrightarrow\!
% a_y$ graviton--photon pair separates from the trace--vector torsion
% channel at the linear level (see~\cref{ChannelStructure} for the
% numerical demonstration on the survey theories) --- and processing
% such a block-diagonal matrix as a single dense object would waste
% both wall time (since the per-mode \lstinline!expm! cost scales as
% $\O(b^3)$ in the block size $b$) and conditioning.
so we compute the block decomposition. Slots $i$ and $j$ are deemed
coupled when either off-diagonal entry $M_{ij}$ or $M_{ji}$ exceeds
$10^{-14}\,\max|M|$ in absolute value,
% \footnote{The kinetic matrix is not in general symmetric in field
% space: derivative cross-couplings between fields of different
% intrinsic parity (e.g.\ scalar--pseudoscalar gradient mixing, or
% graviton--photon mixing in the Gertsenshtein system) contribute
% antisymmetric off-diagonal entries.
% These entries are the structural signature of the parity-odd sector
% and drive the conversion processes that are the focus
% of~\cref{Results}.}
and a union-find~\cite{tarjan1975unionfind} collects the resulting
coupling graph into connected components. The exponential is then
applied block-by-block. The dense per-mode \lstinline!expm! cost
scales as $\O(b^3)$ in the block size
$b$~\cite{higham2009scaling}; splitting a size-$b$ block into $n$
sub-blocks gives
$n\!\times\!\O((b/n)^3)=\O(b^3/n^2)$,
visible in~\cref{fig:PadeBenchmark}, with
conditioning at least as good as the unblocked path.
% The block
% decomposition is retained under the Pad\'e path of the previous
% paragraph for the same reason; the union-find threshold is an
% engineering heuristic and is deliberately conservative.

\paragraph*{Constraint elimination via Schur complement}
We eliminate any algebraic constraints per mode by Schur complement~\cite{boydvandenberghe2004convex} before
forming the exponential. Partitioning the per-mode state
$\hat y(k) = (y_d, y_c)^{\mathsf{T}}$ into a dynamical ($y_d$) and
a constraint part ($y_c$), the equation
$\partial_t \hat y(k) = M(k)\, \hat y(k)$ of~\cref{ModalPerMode} reads
in block form
\begin{align}
  \begin{pmatrix} \partial_t y_d \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} A_{dd}(k) & A_{dc}(k) \\
    \mathsf{S}_{cd}(k) & \mathsf{S}_{cc}(k) \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix} y_d \\ y_c \end{pmatrix}
  \, .
  \label{ModalSchur}
\end{align}
But $\partial_t y_c = 0$, so the constraint row yields
\begin{align}
  y_c = -\mathsf{S}_{cc}^{-1} \mathsf{S}_{cd}\, y_d
  \, ,
  \label{SchurReducedConstraint}
\end{align}
and substitution into the dynamical block produces the reduced
evolution matrix
\begin{align}
  A_{\rm red}(k) = A_{dd}(k) - A_{dc}(k)\, \mathsf{S}_{cc}^{-1}(k)\, \mathsf{S}_{cd}(k)
  \, ,
  \label{SchurReducedMatrix}
\end{align}
on $y_d$ alone.% The matrix $A_{dc}$ here captures references to
% constraint \emph{field values} in dynamical equations.% Absent any
% velocity coupling, $A_{\rm red}$ is the per-mode generator the modal
% exponential acts on.

A second pattern arises when the dynamical block references the
\emph{velocity} of a constraint field. Differentiating
\cref{SchurReducedConstraint} \wrt time yields
$\dot y_c = -\mathsf{S}_{cc}^{-1}\mathsf{S}_{cd}\,\dot y_d$, thus the
dynamical equation
$\dot y_d = A_{\rm red}\,y_d + A_{dc,v}\,\dot y_c$ produces an
\emph{implicit} equation in $\dot y_d$:
\begin{align}
  \left(I \,+\, A_{dc,v}\,\mathsf{S}_{cc}^{-1}\mathsf{S}_{cd}\right)\,
  \dot y_d
  \;=\; A_{\rm red}\, y_d
  \, ,
  \label{SchurImplicitVelocity}
\end{align}
which \TIDAL{} resolves per mode, recovering an explicit generator
$\dot y_d = M_{\rm red}(k)\,y_d$ that the Pad\'e exponential then acts
on. The Schur-complement
reduction is standard DAE
practice~\cite{brenan1996numerical,hairer1996ode2}.

\paragraph*{SVD null-space projection on rank-deficient kinetic matrices}%
\label{SVDNullSpace}
For theories whose linearised kinetic matrix $K$
of~\cref{KineticMatrixDef} is rank-deficient, the redundant directions
of $K$ carry no dynamics. \TIDAL{} identifies its null space and
projects it out by an SVD
truncation~\cite[\S~5.5]{golub2013matrix} before forming the
exponential, leaving a rank-correct generator for the Pad\'e path.
% This is the bit-exact diagnostic that the cross-formulation
% comparison in \cref{Validation} validates against the Proca-vector
% representation.

\paragraph*{Position-dependent coefficients via Krylov action}
Position-dependent coefficients couple the per-mode systems together.
Let $y(x)$ denote any state slot in position space and $c(x)$ its
position-dependent coefficient. On the discrete grid $\{k_n\}_{n=0}^{N-1}$
in Fourier space, this convolution reduces to a finite sum~\cite{cooley1965fft}:
\begin{align}
  (\widehat{c \cdot y})_k
  = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k'} \hat c_{\,k-k'}\, \hat y_{k'}
  \;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\;
  \int \hat c(k - k')\, \hat y(k')\, \diff k'
  \, .
  \label{ModalConvolution}
\end{align}
The per-mode decoupling of~\cref{ModalPerMode} fails: every mode
$\hat y_k$ now receives a contribution from every other mode
$\hat y_{k'}$. The evolution takes the
mode-coupled form
\begin{align}
  \partial_t \hat y_k
  = \sum_{k'} M_{k k'}\, \hat y_{k'}
  \;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\;
  \int M(k, k')\, \hat y(k')\, \diff k'
  \, ,
  \label{ModalCoupled}
\end{align}
with each entry given by
\begin{align}
  M_{k k'} = M(k)\,\delta_{k k'}
  \,+\, \tfrac{1}{N}\,\hat c_{\,k-k'}\,\mathcal{L}(k')
  \, ,
  \label{ModalCoupledEntry}
\end{align}
where $M(k)$ is the per-mode operator
of~\cref{ModalPerMode}, $\hat c_{\,k-k'}$ are the discrete Fourier
coefficients of $c(x)$, and $\mathcal{L}(k') \in \{1,i k',\cdots\}$ is the relevant
Fourier symbol~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev}.

The matrix is dense in general, and is generally non-normal, in which
case the eigendecomposition is inadequate: individual terms
$\exp(\lambda t)$ overflow when $\lambda$ has a large positive real
part, even though the full action $\exp(M t)\, y_0$ stays bounded.%
\footnote{For non-normal $A$ the transient growth of $\|\exp(A t)\|$
is not predicted by the eigenvalues alone; this phenomenon is
analysed by Trefethen \& Embree~\cite[\S~15]{trefethen2005spectra}.}
The matrix-exponential action is computed directly via the
\SciPy{}'s Krylov scheme
\lstinline!expm_multiply!~\cite{almohy2011action} ---
which evaluates $\exp(Mt)\,y_0$ without forming $\exp(Mt)$ explicitly
and stays accurate when $M$ is non-normal.

For localised coefficients the convolution matrix is
effectively banded; \TIDAL{} converts it to compressed-sparse-column
(CSC) format~\cite{demmel1999supernodal,demmel1999superlumt} when the
density falls below 30\%. \Cref{fig:SparseCSCBenchmark}
shows the resulting orders of magnitude speed-up as the matrix sparsifies with
increasing grid resolution.

\paragraph*{Nyquist mode and energy conservation}
% A subtlety in the
% modal solver controls energy conservation at the level of machine
% precision.
A real field has a Hermitian Fourier
transform~\cite{nyquist1928certain,boyd2001chebyshev} thus modes $k$ and
mode $N-k$ are complex conjugates.
% A generic mode is free to be
% complex --- its conjugate partner keeps the field real --- so the
% complex per-mode propagator $\exp(M(k)\,t)$ does it no harm.

The Nyquist mode ($k=N/2$ on even-$N$ grids) is its
own conjugate, so \emph{must} stay real. The gradient
couplings make $\exp(M(k)\,t)$ complex at the Nyquist
wavenumber: each step rotates a fraction of the real amplitude into
the imaginary part, which \lstinline!irfft! then discards on
reconstruction --- the energy carried by that imaginary part is lost.
% (the zero mode is likewise self-conjugate but
% unaffected, the gradient couplings vanishing at $k=0$).

We zero the Nyquist mode
in the initial condition before evolution, which is the linear
analogue of the spectral dealiasing
rule~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev,burns2020dedalus}. With this
projection the relative energy drift decreases monotonically with
resolution in \cref{fig:NyquistEnergy}; without it, the drift grows with $N$ as
the Nyquist mode oscillates faster at finer resolution.

\subsection{Other solvers}%
\label{OtherBackends}

\paragraph*{Pseudo-spectral evaluation}
When a system is ineligible for the modal solver
of~\cref{ModalSolver} but still has periodic spatial boundaries,
\TIDAL{} provides a
\emph{pseudo-spectral} path that the
step-based solvers
%(\IDA{}, \CVODE{}, leapfrog, \SciPy{})
can use: spatial operators are applied as Fourier
multipliers,%~\cite{burns2020dedalus},
\footnote{The pseudo-spectral path uses the exact continuous wavenumber $k$
as a Fourier multiplier --- the same convention as the Fourier modal
solver of~\cref{ModalSolver}. The constraint initial-condition
solver of~\cref{ConstraintIC} matches the active spatial path:
exact $k$ for the pseudo-spectral path, and the FD-stencil dispersion
$k_{\rm mod}^2 = -(2/\Delta x^2)(1-\cos(k\Delta x))$ for the FD path
(with Fornberg generalisations~\cite{fornberg1988generation} at
higher orders), so the constraint IC is spectrally consistent with
the active evolution.}
while the time evolution is driven by the chosen solver.
% \footnote{The path is auto-enabled in every
% solver except \IDA{}, whose Newton iteration uses a sparse
% linear solver to factor the DAE Jacobian $\partial F/\partial y$: the
% FD stencils produce a banded Jacobian that the sparse
% factoring exploits, whereas the spectral path produces a dense
% Jacobian in which every $k$-mode couples to every other, which the
% sparse solver cannot exploit.}
Spectral methods give super-algebraic convergence on smooth periodic
data~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev}: the error decays faster than any power
of $1/N$. \Cref{FDConvergence} shows the spectral path reaching the
round-off floor by $N\approx 32$, while the FD orders display the
predicted algebraic rates.

\paragraph*{\IDA{} --- differential--algebraic equations}
The \IDA{} solver is the implicit differential--algebraic
solver from \SUNDIALS{}~\cite{gardner2022sundials,hindmarsh2005sundials}, accessed via
\lstinline!scikit-sundae!~\cite{randall2024skitsundae}.
\IDA{} integrates a residual form $F(t, y, y') = 0$
% by adaptive backward differentiation formulas.
% of orders $1$ through $5$.
\TIDAL{} populates with $F_i = y_i'- R_i(y)$ for
dynamical fields (with $R_i$ the EL right-hand side),
and with $F_i = R_i(y)$ for constraint fields, so that both classes are
handled simultaneously. \IDA{} is the only \TIDAL{} solver that
solves algebraic constraints at run time: the constraint residual
rows $F_i = R_i(y)$ above are carried in the same Newton iteration as
the differential ones. The Newton iteration needs the Jacobian
$\partial F/\partial y + c_j\,\partial F/\partial y'$ of the residual
at each implicit step; \SUNDIALS{} can finite-difference one from
$F$, but \TIDAL{} supplies an analytical version
(\cref{AnalyticalJacobian}) to avoid that overhead.

\paragraph*{\CVODE{} --- adaptive ODE} The \CVODE{}
solver wraps the \SUNDIALS{} ordinary-differential-equation solver of
the same name~\cite{gardner2022sundials,hindmarsh2005sundials} through
\lstinline!scikit-sundae!~\cite{randall2024skitsundae}.
% , and is
% auto-selected for pure wave systems in which every equation has
% time order~$2$ and a canonical Hamiltonian structure is recorded in
% the specification.
\CVODE{} supports both BDF (for stiff
problems~\cite{hairer1996ode2}) and the Adams family
(non-stiff problems~\cite{hairer1993ode1}).
% Constraint fields, when present in the equation specification but not
% eliminable by the modal Schur path, are frozen at their
% initial-condition values; systems with algebraic constraints therefore
% route to \IDA{} instead.

\paragraph*{Leapfrog --- symplectic St\"ormer--Verlet and Yoshida}
The leapfrog solver implements two symplectic integrators. The first
is the second-order St\"ormer--Verlet
scheme~\cite{verlet1967computer,hairer2006geometric}.
% , with a single force evaluation per timestep.
The second is the fourth-order Yoshida composition of three
St\"ormer--Verlet sub-steps~\cite{yoshida1990symplectic},
% in which a
% negative middle-step weight cancels the leading
% $\O(\Delta t^2)$ error of St\"ormer--Verlet. Adjacent
% half-kicks between the three sub-steps merge into a single combined kick
with the Forest--Ruth fused-kick
scheme~\cite{forestruth1990fourth}.
%, reducing the cost from six force
% evaluations per outer step to three plus one initialiser.
Both integrators preserve a shadow Hamiltonian to
machine precision $\varepsilon_{\rm mach}\approx 2.2\times 10^{-16}$
(IEEE 754 double) and exhibit zero
secular energy drift over arbitrary integration
times~\cite{hairer2006geometric}. It is opt-in via
\lstinline!--scheme leapfrog!, chosen when long-time symplecticity is
the dominant concern.

\paragraph*{\SciPy{} --- general-purpose adaptive ODE} The \SciPy{}
solver wraps \lstinline!scipy.integrate.solve_ivp!~\cite{virtanen2020scipy},
with a default method of DOP853~\cite{hairer1993ode1,dormand1980runge}. On smooth non-stiff problems DOP853 can beat adaptive BDF, since
BDF's per-step implicit solve is only useful for stiff problems. Like leapfrog, the \SciPy{} solver is opt-in only.

\subsection{Initial-condition preparation}%
\label{ICPreparation}

\paragraph*{Constraint projection onto the constraint surface}%
\label{ConstraintIC}
When the equation specification contains
algebraic constraints, the user-supplied initial condition
$(q_0, v_0)$ generically lies off the constraint surface. The initial
condition \emph{must} satisfy these constraints.
\TIDAL{} enforces this by taking the
user-supplied dynamical-slot values $(q_0, v_0)$ as authoritative and
solving the constraints for the constraint-slot
values, producing a self-consistent initial state. The
\lstinline!ensure_consistent_ic! function performs this projection
once, after the initial condition is assembled and before the chosen
solver is invoked.

\paragraph*{Three-tier solver} The selector \lstinline!_select_method!
chooses the fastest applicable solver tier for projection.
The first tier handles the case in which
all boundaries are periodic and the constraint coefficients are
constant: the constraint operator is diagonalised by the discrete
Fourier transform, with wavenumber multipliers matching the
convention of the spatial
operators that drive evolution; the cost is
$\O(N\log N)$~\cite{cooley1965fft}. The second
tier covers non-periodic boundaries and variable coefficients: the
constraint Jacobian is assembled column-by-column by impulse-probing
each operator, then stored as
CSC and factored by SuperLU~\cite{demmel1999supernodal,demmel1999superlumt},
with $\O(N^2)$ build cost and $\O(N)$ solve. The third tier, reserved for
the $N>2\times 10^{5}$ regime, is a Krylov iterative scheme; it is
implemented but not exercised in~\cref{Results},
where the largest grids fit comfortably within tier~$2$.

\paragraph*{Zero-mode regularisation} A pure Poisson constraint on a
periodic domain has a one-dimensional null space: the zero Fourier
mode is undetermined by the elliptic problem and must be supplied by
an external condition. We pin the zero mode to zero as a numerical
regularisation.
% For the gauge-field constraints relevant to the present work,
Physical observables are constructed from
derivatives (the field strengths $F_{\mu\nu}$), and the zero-mode
choice cancels in any gauge-invariant quantity.

\subsection{Analytical Jacobian}%
\label{AnalyticalJacobian}

\paragraph*{Motivation} The implicit solvers \IDA{} and
\CVODE{} both rely on a Jacobian matrix $J = \partial F/\partial y +
c_j\,\partial F/\partial y'$ to drive the Newton iteration of each
backward-differentiation step. FD approximation of
this matrix requires multiple residual evaluations per Newton
iteration~\cite{gardner2022sundials,hindmarsh2005sundials,curtispowellreid1974}. For the multi-field systems with
$N\!\sim\!10^4$ degrees of freedom that arise in the PGT campaigns
of~\cref{Results}, that overhead is the dominant cost.

We precompute the Jacobian analytically, once, from the symbolic
equation specification, and supply
it to the linear solver directly through the \SUNDIALS{} callback
interface~\cite{gardner2022sundials,hindmarsh2005sundials}.

\paragraph*{Three-tier selection} The analytical-Jacobian path
maintains three tiers, selected on the total state-vector size $N$: a
dense tier for $N\le2{,}000$, a sparse tier for
$2{,}000<N\le200{,}000$, and a matrix-free tier for $N>200{,}000$. The dense tier
fills a two-dimensional \NumPy{}~\cite{harris2020numpy} array with $\partial F/\partial y +
c_j\,\partial F/\partial y'$ at every Newton step, which the \SUNDIALS{}
direct linear solver factors by LAPACK LU~\cite{andersonlapack1999}; this path delivers a
substantial wall-time speedup over FD Jacobian
evaluation, with the per-tier comparison shown
in~\cref{fig:AnalyticalJacobianSpeedup}. The sparse tier fills a flat
CSC data buffer with a fixed sparsity
pattern, which \SUNDIALS{} factors with
SuperLU~\cite{demmel1999supernodal,demmel1999superlumt}.
% ; this yields
% a measurable speedup for both solvers, larger for \IDA{}
% (heavier per-step Jacobian cost) than for \CVODE{}.
The matrix-free tier supplies an analytical
Jacobian--vector product callback $J\,v = (\partial F/\partial y)\,v +
c_j\,(\partial F/\partial y')\,v$ to the \SUNDIALS{} Krylov interface,
which then solves the linear step by GMRES~\cite{saad1986gmres}
without ever materialising $J$ explicitly --- the regime where a
finite-difference Jacobian, with its many residual evaluations per
Newton step, would dominate the cost absolutely.

% Thresholds DENSE_THRESHOLD=2000, SPARSE_THRESHOLD=200000, and
% SUPERLU_NNZ_LIMIT=100000 are defined in tidal/solver/_types.py:45--57.
\paragraph*{Assembly cost versus solve cost}
Figure~\ref{fig:AnalyticalJacobianSpeedup} times only the
Jacobian-\emph{fill} stage of each Newton iteration, where the
sparse-CSC callback always wins --- it skips the $\O(N^{2})$
dense \texttt{toarray}, and at small $N$ a single fill is cheaper than
the inner mat-vecs a matrix-free GMRES step would accumulate. The
production tier thresholds, however, are set by the cost of the
\emph{linear solve} that consumes the Jacobian: LAPACK dense LU is
fastest while its working set is cache-resident
($N\!\lesssim\!2{,}000$); SuperLU's sparse LU is fastest while its
fill-in fits in memory
($\mathrm{nnz}\!\lesssim\!10^{5}$~\cite{demmel1999superlumt,demmel1999supernodal});
beyond that the matrix-free GMRES tier avoids the factor entirely.
The dense and sparse tiers thus win on solve cost at their respective
sizes even though the fill timings in
Figure~\ref{fig:AnalyticalJacobianSpeedup} would, on their own,
favour sparse everywhere.

\paragraph*{Circulant operators on periodic grids} When the boundary
conditions are all periodic and the
coefficients are spatially constant: the spatial-operator matrices
are then circulant. We probe a single grid point and tile
the resulting stencil row across all $N$ rows via modular arithmetic,
an $\O(\mathrm{nnz})$ construction in place of the $\O(N^2)$ assembly
of the general sparse path. On the periodic-domain EM
system this contributes an additional factor of roughly $28\times$
in operator-matrix construction.
