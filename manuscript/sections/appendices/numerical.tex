\section{Numerical evolution of the linearised system}%
\label{NumericalEvolution}

\paragraph*{Overview} The numerical stage of \TIDAL{} consumes the
equation specification produced by~\cref{SymbolicComputation} and
evolves the linearised fields on a Cartesian grid. \TIDAL{} is designed
to support arbitrary tensor Lagrangians on either flat or curved
backgrounds --- the example library includes scalar and vector fields
on Minkowski, Klein--Gordon evolution on stereographic $S^2$,
cylindrical coordinates, linearised gravity in transverse--traceless
gauge, and the multi-field Poincar\'e gauge theories that occupy
\cref{Results} --- and the numerical architecture is arranged around
this generality rather than around any specific theory class. The
arrangement consists of a shared substrate (state layout, spatial
operators, coefficient cache) and a dispatcher that selects among
several solver backends from the algebraic structure of the equation
system. The Fourier modal solver of~\cref{ModalSolver} is the primary
backend, and is auto-selected for every survey reported in
\cref{Results}; the supporting infrastructure of state layout, spatial
discretisation, constraint initialisation, and analytical Jacobian is
described in the remaining subsections.

\paragraph*{Solver selection hierarchy} The architectural reason for
multiple backends is that the equation systems \TIDAL{} must handle
form a heterogeneous family. Differential--algebraic equations arising
from gauge sectors require simultaneous algebraic-and-differential
solution; pure wave systems benefit from explicit symplectic
integration when energy conservation matters more than tolerance
control; dissipative parabolic systems break the symplectic structure
and call for implicit methods; and curved-metric systems require
position-dependent coefficient evaluation. A single backend cannot
serve all four. The dispatcher
\lstinline!_resolve_scheme! (in \lstinline!tidal.cli._simulate!)
traverses the eligibility predicates in order, returning the first
match. The full decision tree, including the modal eligibility
sub-checks, is shown in~\cref{SolverDispatchFlowchart}; in summary the
rules are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Modal} when the metric is flat, the boundaries are
    all periodic, the coefficients are time-independent, and every
    right-hand-side operator has an exact Fourier multiplier; algebraic
    constraints, when present, are eliminated by a per-mode Schur
    complement step before the matrix exponential is applied.
  \item \textbf{IDA} (SUNDIALS differential--algebraic, BDF orders
    $1$--$5$~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}) when the modal eligibility
    fails and the system carries equations with
    \texttt{time\_order}~$=0$; algebraic constraints are treated
    natively in the residual form.
  \item \textbf{IDA} when the system carries first-order
    (\texttt{time\_order}~$=1$) equations, since adaptive implicit
    integration is preferred for diffusive and transport equations.
  \item \textbf{IDA} when any right-hand-side carries a
    \texttt{first\_derivative\_t} operator, since dissipation breaks
    the symplectic structure on which a conservative integrator would
    rely.
  \item \textbf{CVODE} (SUNDIALS adaptive ODE,
    BDF~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}) when none of the above predicates
    holds, and the system is a pure second-order wave with a canonical
    Hamiltonian sector recorded in the specification.
  \item \textbf{Leapfrog and \SciPy{}} are available only via
    explicit \texttt{--scheme} override and are never auto-selected;
    they are retained for symplectic long-time evolution
    (\cref{OtherBackends}) and as an independent cross-check of the
    SUNDIALS implementations, respectively.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Equation substrate and state layout}%
\label{EquationSubstrate}

\paragraph*{Euler--Lagrange velocity form} \TIDAL{} stores the
simulation state in the Euler--Lagrange velocity form, comprising
primary fields $q$ and their time derivatives $v_q \equiv \partial_t q$
for second-order equations, together with constraint multipliers
$\Phi$ for fields whose equations of motion are algebraic
(\texttt{time\_order}~$=0$, i.e.\ Lagrange multipliers and gauge
fields with degenerate temporal kinetic term). For a second-order
equation $\partial_t^2 \varphi = L[\varphi]$ on a foliation of
constant-$t$ slices, the substrate carries
$(\varphi, v_\varphi)$ together with the kinematic identity
\begin{align}
  \partial_t \varphi = v_\varphi
  \, ,
  \qquad
  \partial_t v_\varphi = L[\varphi]
  \, ,
  \label{ELVelocityForm}
\end{align}
in the field and velocity slots respectively. The choice of
$(q, v, \Phi)$ rather than the canonical phase-space pair $(q, p)$ is
deliberate: for many of the Lagrangians in our surveys --- those
containing degenerate Hessians, higher-derivative sectors with
Ostrogradsky pathology, or constraint-promotion structures of the
Ja\'en--Llosa--Molina type --- the Legendre transform is either not
unique or genuinely unavailable, and the canonical formulation
becomes an obstruction rather than a convenience. These obstructions
are catalogued in~\cref{ConstraintBarrier}, alongside the structural
analogues in massive gravity (vDVZ, Boulware--Deser) and the
Maxwell--Proca reclassification. Even when the canonical form
\emph{is} available, evaluating the kinetic-matrix inverse at every
right-hand-side application would carry an unnecessary overhead;
the velocity form sidesteps this by recording the velocities directly.
The energy diagnostic of~\cref{Validation} draws on the
\texttt{hamiltonian\_terms} block of the specification independently
of the integration scheme, so that conservation can be monitored
without committing to a Hamiltonian state vector. The relationship
between the $(q, v)$ form and an underlying symplectic structure
where such a structure exists is reviewed in~\cite{hairer2006geometric}.

\paragraph*{State layout and field views} The mapping between a
field's component name and its slot in the flat state vector is
captured by a frozen \texttt{StateLayout} dataclass, which is
constructed once from the equation specification before integration
begins. Every component carries a \texttt{slot\_kind} of one of three
values --- \texttt{"field"} for primary fields, \texttt{"velocity"} for
time derivatives in wave-type equations, or \texttt{"constraint"} for
the multiplier slots --- and an integer time-derivative order. The
flat state itself is wrapped by a \texttt{FieldSet} container that
provides zero-copy named views into the contiguous
\NumPy{}~\cite{harris2020numpy} array, plus a \texttt{rebind} operation
that swaps the underlying buffer for in-place state updates without
reallocation. This is what permits the Yoshida fused-kick scheme of
\cref{OtherBackends} to reuse a single buffer across three force
evaluations per outer timestep, and what allows the modal solver of
\cref{ModalSolver} to share its post-FFT buffer with the constraint
elimination machinery of \cref{ConstraintIC}.

\paragraph*{Coefficient evaluator} Each right-hand-side term carries a
symbolic coefficient string emitted by the \xAct{} stage, which must be
resolved at run time against the user's parameter assignments and, for
backgrounds, the spatial coordinate grid. The
\texttt{CoefficientEvaluator} class implements a four-level cache to
amortise this resolution. The first level stores pre-resolved constants
that depend on neither parameters nor coordinates; the second stores
the parsed parameter-resolved expression; the third stores spatial
grids of coefficient values for position-dependent but time-independent
coefficients (Gaussian magnetic-field profiles, localised plasma
masses, and similar); the fourth performs per-timestep deduplication of
any time-dependent coefficients. The cache also tracks a
periodic-boundary \emph{leak metric} that detects discontinuities at
periodic seams --- a silent failure mode that would otherwise spoil
energy conservation through the integration-by-parts boundary terms in
the Hamiltonian. Because the cache is shared between the
right-hand-side evaluator and the analytical-Jacobian machinery of
\cref{AnalyticalJacobian}, a change to coefficient resolution
propagates automatically to every backend.

\subsection{Spatial discretisation and the operator catalogue}%
\label{SpatialDiscretisation}

\paragraph*{Finite-difference stencils} The default spatial
discretisation is a central-difference scheme on a uniform Cartesian
grid, with stencil width and ghost-cell count controlled by a single
parameter (the \texttt{--fd-order} option, with allowed values $2$,
$4$, and $6$). The orders correspond to widths of $3$, $5$, and $7$
points respectively, with one, two, and three ghost cells per side;
the stencil coefficients are those of Fornberg's algorithm for
arbitrarily spaced grids~\cite{fornberg1988generation}, specialised
here to the uniform-grid limit. At a typical resolution of $N=64$
points the gradient of $\sin(x)$ is computed to a maximum absolute
error of $1.6\times 10^{-3}$ at order $2$, $3.1\times 10^{-6}$ at
order $4$, and $6.4\times 10^{-9}$ at order $6$ --- roughly $500\times$
error reduction per order step at fixed resolution. The default in
the present work is order $4$, which provides $O(\Delta x^4)$
convergence at approximately a ten-percent per-step overhead over
order $2$. Boundary conditions (periodic, Dirichlet, Neumann, or
Robin) are handled uniformly by a ghost-cell formula of the form
$\mathrm{ghost} = \mathrm{const} + \mathrm{factor}\times \mathrm{interior}$,
with recursive layer-by-layer fill for orders greater than~$2$.

\paragraph*{Pseudo-spectral evaluation} When every boundary is
periodic, \TIDAL{} also exposes a pseudo-spectral evaluation path in
which spatial operators are applied as Fourier multipliers via the
real-to-complex FFT \texttt{numpy.fft.rfftn}. In this representation
the identity becomes unity, the gradient $\partial_x$ becomes $ik_x$,
the Laplacian becomes $-k^2$, and the biharmonic operator becomes
$k^4$, with $k = 2\pi\cdot\texttt{rfftfreq}(N,\Delta x)$. The
pseudo-spectral path is auto-enabled for all-periodic boundary
conditions in every solver backend except \texttt{IDA}, where the
differential--algebraic residual form requires the locality of
finite-difference stencils for the sparsity-aware linear solve.
Spectral methods give exponential convergence on smooth periodic
data~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev}; this scaling is realised in practice
on the modal solver's convergence curve in~\cref{SolverConvergence},
which shows the spectral path reaching the round-off floor at
modest resolution while the FD orders display the predicted algebraic
rates. The pseudo-spectral path differs in convention from both the
Fourier modal solver of~\cref{ModalSolver}, which uses the exact
wavenumber $k$ in its evolution matrix, and the constraint
initial-condition solver of~\cref{ConstraintIC}, which uses the
modified wavenumber
$k_{\rm mod}^2 = -(2/\Delta x^2)(1 - \cos(k\,\Delta x))$ at order~$2$,
with order-$4$ and order-$6$ generalisations, so that the constraint
solution remains spectrally consistent with the FD stencils used in
time evolution.

\paragraph*{Operator catalogue} The right-hand side of an equation in
the JSON specification is a list of (coefficient, operator, target
field) triples drawn from a fixed catalogue.~\Cref{OperatorCatalogue}
lists the operators currently registered, partitioned into the
spatial operators used by every backend and the modal-only
classification by time-derivative order. The catalogue is open: new
operators are added by registering an entry in the
\lstinline!_OPERATOR_DECOMP! table (in \lstinline!tidal.solver.modal!)
that specifies the Fourier multiplier and time-derivative order, and
the corresponding ghost-cell stencil in \lstinline!tidal.solver.operators!;
the analytical-Jacobian sparsity pattern and the constraint pre-solver
inherit the registration automatically. Operators with time-derivative
order three or four arise only as source terms in the iterative
perturbative-reduction pipeline of~\cref{PerturbativeReduction},
which is used for higher-derivative Lagrangians, and never in the
second-order base system that the modal solver actually evolves.

\subsection{Fourier modal solver}%
\label{ModalSolver}

\paragraph*{Eligibility and routing} The Fourier modal solver is the
primary backend, and is auto-selected when all five of the following
conditions hold: the metric is flat ($\sqrt{|g|}$ is identically unity,
which the specification records as a \texttt{null} \texttt{volume\_element}
field); every spatial boundary is periodic; the equation system
contains no algebraic constraint that cannot be eliminated by a
Fourier Schur step; every right-hand-side operator has an exact
Fourier multiplier in the modal catalogue; and no coefficient depends
explicitly on time. Position-dependent coefficients are admitted, and
are handled by the Krylov path described below. When the user
requests \texttt{--scheme modal} explicitly on a system that fails one
of the five conditions, the dispatcher raises an error citing the
failed predicate, rather than silently falling back. The exhaustive
eligibility check is implemented as \lstinline!can_use_modal! in
\lstinline!tidal.solver.modal!.

\paragraph*{Mode-by-mode evolution} The state $(q, v)$ is transformed
to Fourier space slot by slot, and the right-hand-side operators are
applied as multiplications by $k$-dependent Fourier symbols. For a
system in which every coefficient is constant, each $k$-mode
decouples, and the evolution reduces to a finite-dimensional linear
ODE on the per-mode state vector~$\hat y(k)$,
\begin{align}
  \frac{\diff}{\diff t}\, \hat y(k) = M(k)\, \hat y(k)
  \, ,
  \qquad
  \hat y(k, t) = \exp\!\big(M(k)\, t\big)\, \hat y(k, 0)
  \, .
  \label{ModalPerMode}
\end{align}
The matrix $M(k)$ has dimension $2 N_{\rm fields}$, e.g.\ $4\times 4$
for a two-field second-order system and $12 \times 12$ for the
six-field Einstein--Maxwell pair of~\cref{Results}; the analogous
matrix for the full quadratic Poincar\'e gauge theory plus
electromagnetism is block-diagonalised by the parity decomposition of
\cref{ChannelStructure} but otherwise has the same structure. The
inverse FFT recovers the time-domain state. The wall time is $O(1)$ in
the integration endpoint $t_{\rm end}$ once the per-mode exponentials
are precomputed: every output snapshot is then a single
matrix--vector product per mode.

\paragraph*{Pad\'e scaling and squaring} The per-mode matrix
exponential in~\cref{ModalPerMode} is computed via the Pad\'e
scaling-and-squaring method of
\texttt{scipy.linalg.expm}~\cite{higham2009scaling}. We do not employ
the eigendecomposition $M(k) = V D V^{-1}$ with $\exp(M t) =
V \exp(D\, t) V^{-1}$ because the eigenvector matrix $V$ becomes
ill-conditioned for several cases of interest in the Poincar\'e
gauge sector: small parameter detunings produce near-degenerate
eigenvalues, and the worst-case condition number
$\operatorname{cond}(V)$ can exceed $10^{13}$ on theories with
cross-coupling. At that conditioning the diagonalisation accumulates
roundoff far above the level at which backward-stable
matrix-exponential algorithms remain
accurate~\cite{higham2008functions,molervanloan2003nineteen,golub2013matrix,trefethen1997numerical};
\cite{higham2008functions}~\S 2.3 records the cond-$V$ abandonment
threshold above which diagonalisation should be replaced by a
backward-stable scheme, and we adopt that recommendation. The Pad\'e
path is by contrast robust at arbitrary $\operatorname{cond}(V)$. The
wall-time penalty for adopting it is modest: on three representative
campaign theories the ratio of Pad\'e wall time to the former
eigendecomposition wall time lies in the interval $0.49$--$1.06$
(\cref{tab:PadeBenchmark}), so we incur no significant performance
cost in exchange for the robustness guarantee. The eigendecomposition
path remains available internally for the closed-form Duhamel kernel
used in the perturbative-reduction pipeline
of~\cref{PerturbativeReduction}, which requires the per-eigenmode
expansion explicitly.

\paragraph*{Block-aware union-find} The evolution matrix $M(k)$
inherits the block structure of the underlying coupled-field
equations. A typical multi-field theory decomposes into several
decoupled sub-blocks --- for example, the $h_\times\!\leftrightarrow\!
a_y$ graviton--photon pair separates from the trace--vector torsion
channel at the linear level --- and processing such a block-diagonal
matrix as a single dense object would waste both wall time (since the
per-mode \texttt{expm} cost scales as $O(b^3)$ in the block size $b$)
and conditioning. We therefore precompute the block decomposition:
slots $i$ and $j$ are deemed coupled if $|M_{ij}|$ exceeds a heuristic
cut-off of $10^{-14}\!\cdot\!\max|M|$ or $|M_{ji}|$ exceeds the same
cut-off, and a union-find with path compression collects the resulting
graph into connected components. The exponential is then applied
block-by-block: a single dense $12\!\times\!12$ block resolved into
two independent $6\!\times\!6$ blocks saves a factor of four in the
per-mode cost, and we observe both the speed-up and an unconditional
improvement in numerical conditioning. The block decomposition is
retained under the Pad\'e path of the previous paragraph for the same
reason; the union-find threshold is an engineering heuristic and is
deliberately conservative.

\paragraph*{Constraint elimination via Fourier Schur complement}
When the equation system carries algebraic constraints
(\texttt{time\_order}~$=0$ fields) and is otherwise modal-eligible,
we eliminate the constraint slots per mode by Schur complement before
forming the exponential. Partitioning the per-mode evolution matrix
into a dynamical block $A$ and a constraint block,
\begin{align}
  M(k) = \begin{pmatrix} A(k) & \mathsf{S}_{dc}(k) \\
    \mathsf{S}_{cd}(k) & \mathsf{S}_{cc}(k) \end{pmatrix}
  \, ,
  \label{ModalSchur}
\end{align}
the algebraic block $\mathsf{S}_{cd}\, y_d + \mathsf{S}_{cc}\, y_c = 0$
yields $y_c = -\mathsf{S}_{cc}^{-1}\!\mathsf{S}_{cd}\, y_d$, and
substitution into the dynamical block produces a reduced evolution
matrix on $y_d$ alone. For systems with a rank-deficient kinetic
matrix (degenerate kinetic eigenstructures, gauge fields with
Stueckelberg-type couplings, the Maxwell sector with a manifestly
gauge-symmetric quadratic form) the same reduced exponential is
evaluated through the generalised eigenvalue problem
$(K - \lambda M)\, v = 0$, which is solved internally by the QZ
algorithm of Moler \& Stewart~\cite{moler1973algorithm} as exposed by
\texttt{scipy.linalg.eig(A, B)}. QZ handles rank-deficiency cleanly
through the Schur form without requiring the eigenvector matrix to be
well conditioned. The Schur-complement reduction itself is standard
DAE practice~\cite{brenan1996numerical,hairer1996ode2}, and a unified
entry point shared by both the modal solver and the constraint
initial-condition machinery of~\cref{ConstraintIC} ensures that the
same Schur operator is used in both contexts.

\paragraph*{Position-dependent coefficients via Krylov action}
Position-dependent coefficients --- a Gaussian magnetic-field profile
$\Bzero(x)$, a localised plasma mass term, a coordinate-dependent
gauge-fixing source --- couple the per-mode systems together. The
product $c(x)\, u(x)$ becomes a Fourier convolution
$\hat c \ast \hat u$, and the evolution matrix becomes a single large
block of dimension $N_{\rm modes}\!\times\!N_{\rm slots}$. The
convolution matrix is generally non-normal, in which case the
eigendecomposition picture breaks down for a fundamental reason
rather than a numerical one: individual eigenvalues $\exp(\lambda t)$
overflow even when the action $\exp(M t)\, y_0$ is bounded. This is
the pseudospectral phenomenon analysed in Trefethen \&
Embree~\cite{trefethen2005spectra}~\S 15, where the transient growth
of $\|\exp(A t)\|$ for non-normal $A$ is controlled by the
$\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum rather than by the spectrum itself. We
bypass the eigendecomposition entirely on this path and compute the
action of the matrix exponential directly via the Krylov scheme of
\texttt{scipy.sparse.linalg.expm\_multiply}~\cite{almohy2011action}.
For localised coefficient profiles the Fourier convolution kernel
decays exponentially in mode separation, so the convolution matrix is
effectively banded; \TIDAL{} converts it to sparse CSC format when
the density falls below a heuristic thirty-percent cut-off, and the
resulting per-snapshot cost drops by a factor of $23$--$27\times$
over the dense path at grid sizes $N\in\{128, 256, 512\}$.

\paragraph*{Nyquist mode and energy conservation} A subtlety in the
modal solver controls energy conservation at the level of machine
precision. The real-to-complex FFT's Nyquist bin (the $k=N/2$ mode on
even-$N$ grids) must be purely real for real-valued fields, but the
per-mode evolution matrix has complex entries from the gradient
couplings, so $\exp(M t)$ generates an imaginary Nyquist component
which \texttt{irfft} silently discards on each reconstruction. We
zero the Nyquist mode in the initial condition before evolution,
which is the linear analogue of the dealiasing rule used by spectral
codes for nonlinear
problems~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev,burns2020dedalus}. With the Nyquist
zero in place we observe a relative energy drift of
$|\diff E/E|\approx 2\times 10^{-14}$ on \texttt{coupled\_scalars} at
every resolution from $N=128$ to $N=1024$, and $|\diff E/E|\approx
1.3\times 10^{-14}$ on the full six-field Einstein--Maxwell system;
without the Nyquist zero, the same systems exhibit a
resolution-independent drift of order $10^{-5}$. The convergence of
the modal solver against the analytic Boccaletti baseline
of~\cite{boccaletti1970conversion} is documented
in~\cref{SolverConvergence} and discussed in~\cref{Validation}.

\subsection{Other backends}%
\label{OtherBackends}

\paragraph*{\texttt{IDA} --- differential--algebraic equations}
The \texttt{IDA} backend is the implicit differential--algebraic
solver from SUNDIALS~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}, accessed via the
\texttt{scikit-sundae}~\cite{mekki2023skitsundae} \Python{} wrapper.
\texttt{IDA} integrates a residual form $F(t, y, y') = 0$ by adaptive
backward differentiation formulas of orders $1$ through $5$.
\TIDAL{} populates the residual with $F_i = y_i'- R_i(y)$ for
dynamical fields (with $R_i$ the Euler--Lagrange right-hand side),
and with $F_i = R_i(y)$ for constraint fields, so that a single
Newton iteration handles both classes simultaneously. The
\texttt{IDA} backend is auto-selected whenever the modal solver is
ineligible and a constraint, first-order, or dissipative equation is
present, and is the only \TIDAL{} backend that supports algebraic
constraints natively at run time. We pair it with the analytical
Jacobian of~\cref{AnalyticalJacobian}, and use the default tolerances
\texttt{rtol}=$10^{-8}$, \texttt{atol}=$10^{-10}$ throughout the
campaigns of~\cref{Results}.

\paragraph*{\texttt{CVODE} --- adaptive ODE} The \texttt{CVODE}
backend wraps the SUNDIALS ordinary-differential-equation solver of
the same name~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials} through
\texttt{scikit-sundae}~\cite{mekki2023skitsundae}, and is
auto-selected for pure wave systems in which every equation has
time order~$2$ and a canonical Hamiltonian structure is recorded in
the specification. \texttt{CVODE} supports both
backward-differentiation formulas (orders $1$ through $5$, suitable
for the mild stiffness that finite-difference discretisations
introduce through eigenvalues scaling as $1/\Delta x^2$) and the
Adams family (orders $1$ through $12$, for non-stiff problems).
Constraint fields, when present in the equation specification but not
eliminable by the modal Schur path, are frozen at their
initial-condition values --- a deliberate limitation that motivates
the auto-dispatch to \texttt{IDA} when any algebraic constraint is
detected.

\paragraph*{Leapfrog --- symplectic St\"ormer--Verlet and Yoshida}
The leapfrog backend implements two symplectic integrators. The first
is the second-order St\"ormer--Verlet scheme, with a single force
evaluation per timestep. The second is the fourth-order Yoshida
composition~\cite{yoshida1990symplectic},
\begin{align}
  S_4(\Delta t) = S_2(w_1\,\Delta t) \circ S_2(w_2\,\Delta t)
  \circ S_2(w_1\,\Delta t)
  \, ,
  \quad
  w_1 = \frac{1}{2 - 2^{1/3}}\, ,
  \quad
  w_2 = -\,\frac{2^{1/3}}{2 - 2^{1/3}}\, ,
  \label{YoshidaWeights}
\end{align}
in which the negative middle weight cancels the leading
$O(\Delta t^2)$ error of St\"ormer--Verlet. Adjacent half-kicks
between the three sub-steps in~\cref{YoshidaWeights} merge into a
single combined kick (the Forest--Ruth fused-kick scheme), reducing
the cost from six force evaluations per outer step to three plus one
initialiser. Both integrators preserve a shadow Hamiltonian to
machine precision and exhibit zero secular energy drift over arbitrary
integration times~\cite{hairer2006geometric}. We retain the leapfrog
backend as an opt-in scheme (\texttt{--scheme leapfrog}) for long-time
conservative runs in which the energy-drift guarantee matters more
than tolerance control; the dispatcher never selects it automatically,
because the adaptive solvers are a safer default on systems whose
stability properties are not yet understood.

\paragraph*{\SciPy{} --- general-purpose adaptive ODE} The \SciPy{}
backend wraps \texttt{scipy.integrate.solve\_ivp}~\cite{virtanen2020scipy},
with a default method of DOP853 (an eighth-order embedded
Runge--Kutta scheme~\cite{dormand1980runge}) and the Radau and
BDF families available through \texttt{--method}. We retain this
backend for two reasons. First, smooth non-stiff problems are
sometimes solved more cheaply by an explicit high-order method than by
adaptive BDF, because DOP853 requires fewer function evaluations per
unit of accuracy. Second, the existence of a second independent
implementation --- one routed through SUNDIALS and one through
\SciPy{} --- provides a cross-check during validation: a result that
agrees between two independent integrators carries more weight than a
result that only agrees with itself. Like leapfrog, the \SciPy{}
backend is opt-in only.

\subsection{Constraint initialisation}%
\label{ConstraintIC}

\paragraph*{Motivation} When the equation specification contains
algebraic constraints, the user-supplied initial condition
$(q_0, v_0)$ generically lies off the constraint surface. The Maxwell
$A_0$ satisfies an instantaneous Helmholtz equation in the Lorenz
gauge ($\nabla^2 A_0 - m^2 A_0 = $ source), the linearised
gravitational-wave metric satisfies a transversality condition, and
similar constraints arise from every gauge-fixed sector of the
symbolic pipeline (see~\cref{Architecture}). Without projection onto
the constraint surface the \texttt{IDA} Newton iteration fails to
converge at the first timestep, and the modal Schur path produces an
inconsistent reduced state. The \lstinline!ensure_consistent_ic!
function in \lstinline!tidal.solver.constraint_solve! performs this
projection once, after the user-specified initial condition has been
built and before the chosen backend is invoked.

\paragraph*{Three-tier solver} The projection is implemented as a
three-tier solver. The first tier handles the common case in which
all boundaries are periodic and the constraint coefficients are
constant: the constraint operator is diagonalised by the discrete
Fourier transform, with FD-order-aware modified wavenumbers
$k_{\rm mod}^2 = -(2/\Delta x^2)(1 - \cos(k\,\Delta x))$ at order~$2$
and the analogous higher-order expressions at orders~$4$ and~$6$ so
that the constraint solution is spectrally consistent with the FD
stencils used during evolution; the cost is $O(N\log N)$. The second
tier covers non-periodic boundaries and variable coefficients: the
constraint Jacobian is built by impulse-probing the same
\texttt{RHSEvaluator} operators used during evolution, then stored as
sparse CSC and factored by SuperLU~\cite{demmel1999supernodal} with
$O(N^2)$ build cost and $O(N)$ solve. The third tier, reserved for
the $N>2\times 10^{5}$ regime, is a Krylov iterative scheme; it is
implemented but not exercised in the campaigns of~\cref{Results},
where the largest grids fit comfortably within tier~$2$. The
dispatcher \lstinline!_select_method! chooses the fastest applicable
tier from the boundary conditions and coefficient structure recorded
in the specification.

\paragraph*{Zero-mode regularisation} A pure Poisson constraint on a
periodic domain has a one-dimensional null space: the zero Fourier
mode is undetermined by the elliptic problem and must be supplied by
an external condition. We pin the zero mode to zero as a numerical
regularisation, because the physical observables of the present work
depend on derivatives of the constraint field (the electric and
magnetic field strengths, not the four-potential) and so are
insensitive to this choice. This is a numerical gauge fix, distinct
from the algebraic gauge-fixing presets implemented at the symbolic
stage and discussed in~\cref{GaugeFixing}.

\subsection{Analytical Jacobian}%
\label{AnalyticalJacobian}

\paragraph*{Motivation} The implicit backends \texttt{IDA} and
\texttt{CVODE} both rely on a Jacobian matrix $J = \partial F/\partial y +
c_j\,\partial F/\partial y'$ to drive the Newton iteration of each
backward-differentiation step. Finite-difference approximation of
this matrix --- the default behaviour of every adaptive solver out
of the box --- requires $O(n_{\rm colors})$ residual evaluations per
Newton iteration, where $n_{\rm colors}$ is the chromatic number of
the sparsity graph. For the multi-field systems with
$N\!\sim\!10^4$ degrees of freedom that arise in the PGT campaigns
of~\cref{Results}, that overhead is the dominant cost. We circumvent
it by precomputing the Jacobian analytically, once, from the symbolic
right-hand-side terms in the equation specification, and supplying
it to the linear solver directly through the SUNDIALS callback
interface of~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}.

\paragraph*{Three-tier dispatch} The analytical-Jacobian machinery
maintains three distinct delivery tiers, with thresholds $N \leq
2{,}000$ for the dense tier, $2{,}000<N\leq 200{,}000$ for the sparse
tier, and $N>200{,}000$ for the matrix-free tier. The dense tier
fills a two-dimensional \NumPy{} array with $\partial F/\partial y +
c_j\,\partial F/\partial y'$ at every Newton step, which the SUNDIALS
direct linear solver factors by LAPACK LU; this path delivers a
five-fold wall-time speedup over finite differencing on
representative small systems. The sparse tier instead fills a flat
compressed-sparse-column data buffer paired with a fixed sparsity
pattern, which SUNDIALS factors with
SuperLU~\cite{demmel1999supernodal}; this yields a roughly
$2.5\times$ speedup for \texttt{IDA} and $1.2$--$1.4\times$ for
\texttt{CVODE}. The matrix-free tier supplies an analytical
Jacobian--vector product callback $J\,v = (\partial F/\partial y)\,v +
c_j\,(\partial F/\partial y')\,v$ to the SUNDIALS Krylov interface,
which then solves the linear step by GMRES~\cite{saad1986gmres}
without ever materialising $J$ explicitly --- this is the regime in
which the $O(n_{\rm colors})$ overhead of finite differencing would
dominate absolutely.

\paragraph*{Coordinate accumulation} The sparse Jacobian matrix is
built by accumulating contributions from each operator into a
coordinate (COO) triplet buffer, then converting once at the end to
compressed-sparse-column format. We adopted this scheme after the
profiler identified the naive list-of-lists assembly as the
bottleneck: at a $48\!\times\!48$ grid the LIL-based assembly took
$1{,}700\,\mathrm{ms}$ per Jacobian, while the COO path takes
$4\,\mathrm{ms}$, a more than four-hundred-fold improvement that
brought Jacobian construction down from the dominant cost to a
negligible fraction of the per-step budget. The cost scales linearly
with the number of non-zero entries rather than with the system size,
which is what permits the $N\sim 10^4$ surveys of~\cref{Results} to
run within the wall-clock budgets we report.

\paragraph*{Circulant operators on periodic grids} A further saving
is available when the boundary conditions are all periodic and the
coefficients are spatially constant: the spatial-operator matrices
are then circulant, since each row is a cyclic shift of the same
stencil. We exploit this by probing a single grid point and tiling
the resulting stencil row across all $N$ rows via modular arithmetic,
an $O(\mathrm{nnz})$ construction in place of the $O(N^2)$ assembly
of the general sparse path. On the periodic-domain Einstein--Maxwell
system this contributes an additional factor of roughly $28\times$
in operator-matrix construction.

\paragraph*{Substrate sharing} The analytical Jacobian reuses the
same \texttt{CoefficientEvaluator} cache and operator catalogue that
the \texttt{RHSEvaluator} draws from in time evolution. A change to
coefficient resolution therefore propagates to all three tiers
without duplication, and an operator added to the catalogue acquires
Jacobian support automatically once its sparsity offsets are
registered. This architectural sharing is what permits the
engineering layer to scale with the operator catalogue as new
theories are added.

\subsection{Dispatch and runtime workflow}%
\label{Dispatch}

\paragraph*{JSON specification ingestion} The numerical stage begins
by parsing the JSON specification produced
by~\cref{SymbolicComputation} into an \texttt{EquationSystem}
dataclass. The parser records each equation's left-hand-side
time-derivative order, the list of right-hand-side (coefficient,
operator, target field) triples, the Hamiltonian terms used by the
energy diagnostic, and the mass and coupling matrices used by the
modal solver. From this representation \lstinline!StateLayout.from_spec!
builds the slot mapping of~\cref{EquationSubstrate},
\texttt{CoefficientEvaluator} precomputes the four-level cache, and
the operator catalogue of~\cref{SpatialDiscretisation} resolves each
operator string to a \NumPy{} callable.

\paragraph*{Auto-dispatch} The six-rule auto-selection algorithm of
the section opener is implemented as \lstinline!_resolve_scheme!
(in \lstinline!tidal.cli._simulate!), as shown
in~\cref{SolverDispatchFlowchart}. The function evaluates each
predicate in turn and returns a backend tag, which is then used to
instantiate the appropriate solver class. Manual override is
available through the \texttt{--scheme} command-line option and takes
precedence over auto-selection, with one exception: an explicit
request for \texttt{--scheme modal} on a system that fails
\lstinline!can_use_modal! raises an error citing the failed
predicate, because falling back silently to a different solver would
mask the unmet eligibility condition.

\paragraph*{Result handling} Every backend returns a uniform result
dictionary with snapshot times, the corresponding flat state vectors,
a success boolean, and a human-readable status string; the
unification across the SUNDIALS~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials} and
\SciPy{}~\cite{virtanen2020scipy} backends is provided by a thin
adapter layer in each solver module. The
\lstinline!tidal.io._writer! module serialises these to the output
directory together with the resolved equation specification and the
runtime parameter values, so that downstream measurement and
analysis subcommands (\texttt{measure}, \texttt{analyze},
\texttt{plot}) can reconstruct the run without re-reading the
original \xAct{} session. The architecture diagram
of~\cref{Architecture} summarises this two-stage pipeline at the
level of data flow; the present appendix has filled in its
right-hand half.
