\section{Numerical evolution of the linearised system}%
\label{NumericalEvolution}

The numerical stage of \TIDAL{} consumes the
equation specification produced by~\cref{SymbolicComputation} and
evolves the linearised fields on a Cartesian sampling grid,
supporting either flat or curved spacetime metrics. The grid and
field-container design draws on the py-pde
framework~\cite{zwicker2020pypde}. \TIDAL{} is designed for arbitrary
tensor Lagrangians on flat or curved backgrounds; validation against
analytic limits is collected in~\cref{Validation}, and the
symbolic-stage outputs for the surveyed theories are tabulated
in~\cref{SymbolicOutputs}. \TIDAL{} provides a shared substrate ---
state layout, spatial operators, coefficient cache --- and a
dispatcher that selects among several solver backends from the
algebraic structure of the equation system. The Fourier modal solver
of~\cref{ModalSolver} is the primary backend, and is auto-selected
for every survey reported in~\cref{Results}.

\paragraph*{Solver selection hierarchy} \TIDAL{} provides several
solver backends because the equation systems it handles form a
heterogeneous family. Differential--algebraic equations from gauge
sectors require simultaneous algebraic-and-differential
solution~\cite{henneaux1992quantization}; pure wave systems benefit
from explicit symplectic integration when long-time energy
conservation matters more than tolerance control; and curved-metric
systems require position-dependent coefficient evaluation. The
dispatcher \lstinline!_resolve_scheme! selects the first applicable
backend from the following ordered rules; the full decision tree,
including the modal eligibility sub-checks, is shown
in~\cref{SolverDispatchFlowchart}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Modal} when the metric is flat, the boundaries are
    all periodic, and the coefficients are time-independent;
    algebraic constraints, when present, are eliminated per-mode by
    Schur complement before the exponential (\cref{ModalSolver}).
  \item \textbf{IDA} (\SUNDIALS{} differential--algebraic, BDF orders
    $1$--$5$~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}) when the modal eligibility
    fails and the system carries equations with
    \lstinline!time_order!~$=0$; algebraic constraints are handled in
    the residual form described in~\cref{OtherBackends}.
  \item \textbf{IDA} when the system carries first-order
    (\lstinline!time_order!~$=1$) equations, since adaptive implicit
    integration is preferred for diffusive and transport equations.
  \item \textbf{IDA} when any right-hand-side carries a
    \lstinline!first_derivative_t! operator (dissipation, handled by
    implicit BDF rather than the symplectic path).
  \item \textbf{CVODE} (\SUNDIALS{} adaptive ODE,
    BDF~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}) when none of the above
    predicates holds and the system is a pure second-order wave.
  \item \textbf{Leapfrog and \SciPy{}} are available only via
    explicit \lstinline!--scheme! override and are never auto-selected;
    they are retained for symplectic long-time evolution
    (\cref{OtherBackends}) and as an independent cross-check of the
    \SUNDIALS{} implementations, respectively.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Equation substrate and state layout}%
\label{EquationSubstrate}

\paragraph*{State representation} \TIDAL{} represents the simulation
state at any given time as a flat vector of numerical values. The
equations of motion come from the JSON specification produced
by~\cref{SymbolicComputation} (format illustrated in~\cref{JSONExample};
each right-hand side is a list of (coefficient, operator, target
field) triples) and are
applied to the state by the right-hand-side evaluator at every
timestep. The number of slots a
field contributes to this state vector is determined by the
time-derivative order of its equation of motion:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Wave fields} (\lstinline!time_order!~$=2$) contribute
    two slots: an array of values for the field $q$ itself, and an
    array of values for its time derivative
    $v_q \equiv \partial_t q$ at the current time. The two arrays
    are linked at evaluation time by the kinematic
    identity~\cref{ELVelocityForm} below, which the right-hand-side
    evaluator uses to advance the field slot from the velocity slot.
  \item \textbf{First-order fields} (\lstinline!time_order!~$=1$)
    contribute one slot: an array of values for the field. The
    integrator advances this array using $\partial_t q$ computed from
    the right-hand-side specification at each timestep.
  \item \textbf{Constraint fields} (\lstinline!time_order!~$=0$)
    contribute one slot: an array of values for the field. The values
    are not advanced by an integrator at all; they are determined at
    each timestep by solving the field's algebraic equation of motion
    --- typically a Helmholtz or Poisson equation --- against the
    current values of the other fields in the system.
\end{enumerate}
A familiar example of the constraint class is the Maxwell scalar
potential $A_0$: in the Lorenz gauge it does not propagate as a wave
but is instead determined at each instant by an instantaneous
Helmholtz equation $(\nabla^2 - m^2)\, A_0 = \mathcal{S}[A_i]$, with
the source $\mathcal{S}[A_i]$ supplied by the spatial photon
components. For a wave field, the kinematic identity is
\begin{align}
  \partial_t \varphi = v_\varphi
  \, ,
  \qquad
  \partial_t v_\varphi = L[\varphi, v_\varphi]
  \, ,
  \label{ELVelocityForm}
\end{align}
in the field and velocity slots respectively. The Euler--Lagrange
right-hand side $L$ in general acts on both the field and its time
derivative.

\paragraph*{Velocity form versus canonical phase space} Writing the
quadratic kinetic operator $\mathcal{K}$ of~\cref{KineticMatrix} as a
polynomial in time derivatives, its leading $\partial_t^2$ block is
the kinetic matrix $K_{ij}$, defined as the Hessian of the linearised
Lagrangian density with respect to the field
velocities~\cite{henneaux1992quantization},
\begin{align}
  K_{ij} \equiv \frac{\partial^2 \lag}{\partial(\partial_t \varphi_i)\,
    \partial(\partial_t \varphi_j)}
  \, ,
\end{align}
evaluated at the background. The canonical momenta are defined by
differentiation of the Lagrangian with respect to the velocities,
\begin{align}
  p_i \equiv \frac{\partial \lag}{\partial(\partial_t \varphi_i)}
  \, ,
  \label{CanonicalMomenta}
\end{align}
which for a Lagrangian quadratic in time derivatives gives the linear
relation $p_i = K_{ij}\, v_j$. To express the Hamiltonian density
$\ham = p_i\, v_i - \lag$ in terms of the canonical pair $(q, p)$
alone --- as the Hamiltonian formulation requires --- one must
eliminate the velocities through $v_i = (K^{-1})_{ij}\, p_j$. The
inversion fails precisely when $K$ is singular at the background; such
failures may originate from gauge redundancy, from genuine
degeneracies in the field-space kinetic structure, or from kinematic
degeneracies in the base theory that the perturbative-reduction
scheme of~\cref{ConstraintBarrier} then promotes to dynamical fields
when the small parameter is turned on. In each case the canonical
formulation requires explicit constrained-Hamiltonian
treatment~\cite{henneaux1992quantization}; the failure modes
relevant to the present surveys are catalogued
in~\cref{ConstraintBarrier}. Even when the canonical form
\emph{is} available, evaluating $K^{-1}$ at every right-hand-side
application would carry an unnecessary overhead; the velocity form
sidesteps this by recording $v_q$ directly. The energy diagnostic
of~\cref{Validation} draws on the \lstinline!hamiltonian_terms! block
of the specification independently of the integration scheme, so that
conservation can be monitored without committing to a Hamiltonian
state vector.

\paragraph*{State layout and field views} The mapping between a
field's component name and its slot in the flat state vector is
captured by a frozen \lstinline!StateLayout! dataclass~\cite{zwicker2020pypde},
constructed once from the equation specification before integration
begins. Each component carries a \lstinline!slot_kind! discriminator
drawn from the three categories enumerated above. The flat state
itself is wrapped by a \lstinline!FieldSet!
container~\cite{zwicker2020pypde} that provides zero-copy named views
into the contiguous \NumPy{}~\cite{harris2020numpy} array, plus a
\lstinline!rebind! operation that re-points the container's named
views at a different underlying buffer without copying. The pattern
follows the field-container design of
py-pde~\cite{zwicker2020pypde}; for the multi-substep symplectic
schemes of~\cref{OtherBackends} the rebind avoids per-substep
allocation~\cite{yoshida1990symplectic,forestruth1990fourth}.

\paragraph*{Coefficient evaluator} Each right-hand-side term carries a
symbolic coefficient string emitted by the \xAct{} stage, which must be
resolved at run time against the user's parameter assignments and, for
backgrounds, the spatial coordinate grid. The
\lstinline!CoefficientEvaluator! class implements a four-level cache to
amortise these per-call evaluations. The first level stores pre-resolved constants
that depend on neither parameters nor coordinates; the second stores
the parsed parameter-resolved expression; the third stores spatial
grids of coefficient values for position-dependent but time-independent
coefficients (Gaussian magnetic-field profiles, localised plasma
masses, and similar); the fourth performs per-timestep deduplication of
any time-dependent coefficients.\footnote{The cache also tracks a
periodic-boundary leak metric that detects discontinuities at periodic
seams. Without this guard, integration-by-parts boundary terms in the
Hamiltonian density would silently spoil long-time energy
conservation.}

\subsection{Spatial discretisation and the operator catalogue}%
\label{SpatialDiscretisation}

\paragraph*{Finite-difference stencils} The default spatial
discretisation is a central-difference scheme on a uniform Cartesian
grid, with stencil width and ghost-cell count controlled by a single
parameter (the \lstinline!--fd-order! option, with allowed values $2$,
$4$, and $6$). The orders correspond to widths of $3$, $5$, and $7$
points respectively, with one, two, and three ghost cells per side;
the stencil coefficients are those of Fornberg's algorithm for
arbitrarily spaced grids~\cite{fornberg1988generation}, specialised
here to the uniform-grid limit. The expected algebraic convergence
rates $N^{-2}$, $N^{-4}$, $N^{-6}$ are realised on a smooth gradient
test in~\cref{FDConvergence}. Boundary conditions (periodic,
Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin) are handled uniformly by a ghost-cell
formula of the form $\mathrm{ghost} = \mathrm{const} +
\mathrm{factor}\times \mathrm{interior}$~\cite{leveque2007finite}.
The finite-difference path is the fallback for systems on which
neither the modal solver of~\cref{ModalSolver} nor the pseudo-spectral
path below is eligible.

\paragraph*{Pseudo-spectral evaluation} When every boundary is
periodic, \TIDAL{} also exposes a pseudo-spectral evaluation path in
which spatial operators are applied as Fourier multipliers via the
real-to-complex FFT \lstinline!numpy.fft.rfftn!, with
with wavenumbers $k_n = 2\pi n/(N\,\Delta x)$~\cite{burns2020dedalus}.
The pseudo-spectral path is auto-enabled for all-periodic boundary
conditions in every solver backend except \IDA{}, whose Newton
iteration uses a sparse linear solver to factor the DAE Jacobian
$\partial F/\partial y$: the finite-difference stencils produce a
banded Jacobian that the sparse factoring exploits, whereas the
spectral path produces a dense Jacobian in which every $k$-mode
couples to every other, which the sparse solver cannot exploit.
Spectral methods give exponential convergence on smooth periodic
data~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev}; this scaling is realised in practice
on the modal solver's convergence curve in~\cref{SolverConvergence},
which shows the spectral path reaching the round-off floor at modest
resolution while the FD orders display the predicted algebraic rates.
The pseudo-spectral path differs in convention from both the Fourier
modal solver of~\cref{ModalSolver}, which uses the exact wavenumber
$k$ in its evolution matrix, and the constraint initial-condition
solver of~\cref{ConstraintIC}, which uses the modified wavenumber
$k_{\rm mod}^2 = -(2/\Delta x^2)(1 - \cos(k\,\Delta x))$ at order~$2$,
with the order-$4$ and order-$6$ generalisations of the Fornberg
stencils~\cite{fornberg1988generation}, so that the constraint
solution remains spectrally consistent with the FD stencils used in
time evolution.

\subsection{Fourier modal solver}%
\label{ModalSolver}

\paragraph*{Eligibility and routing} The Fourier modal solver is the
primary backend, and is auto-selected when all five of the following
conditions hold: the metric is flat ($\sqrt{|g|}$ is identically unity,
which the specification records as a \lstinline!null! \lstinline!volume_element!
field); every spatial boundary is periodic; the equation system
contains no algebraic constraint that cannot be eliminated by a
Fourier Schur step; every right-hand-side operator has an exact
Fourier multiplier; and no coefficient depends explicitly on time.

\paragraph*{Mode-by-mode evolution} The state $(q, v)$ is transformed
to Fourier space slot by slot, and the right-hand-side operators are
applied as multiplications by $k$-dependent Fourier symbols. For a
system in which every coefficient is constant, each $k$-mode
decouples, and the evolution reduces to a finite-dimensional linear
ODE on the per-mode state vector~$\hat y(k)$,
\begin{align}
  \frac{\diff}{\diff t}\, \hat y(k) = M(k)\, \hat y(k)
  \, ,
  \qquad
  \hat y(k, t) = \exp\!\big(M(k)\, t\big)\, \hat y(k, 0)
  \, .
  \label{ModalPerMode}
\end{align}
The matrix $M(k)$ has dimension $2 N_{\rm fields}$, e.g.\ $4\times 4$
for a two-field second-order system and $12 \times 12$ for the
six-field Einstein--Maxwell pair of~\cref{Results}; the analogous
matrix for the full quadratic Poincar\'e gauge theory plus
electromagnetism is block-diagonalised by the parity decomposition of
\cref{ChannelStructure} but otherwise has the same structure. The
wall time is $\mathcal{O}(1)$ in the integration endpoint $t_{\rm end}$ once
the per-mode exponentials are precomputed: every output snapshot is
then a single matrix--vector product per mode. When some coefficients
are position-dependent, the per-mode decoupling fails and the
evolution is computed instead by Krylov action on the full
mode-coupled matrix; that path is described below.

\paragraph*{Pad\'e scaling and squaring} The per-mode matrix
exponential in~\cref{ModalPerMode} is computed via the Pad\'e
scaling-and-squaring method of
\lstinline!scipy.linalg.expm!~\cite{higham2009scaling}. We do not
employ the eigendecomposition $M(k) = V D V^{-1}$ with
$\exp(M t) = V \exp(D\, t) V^{-1}$ because the eigenvector matrix $V$
becomes ill-conditioned for several cases of interest in the
Poincar\'e gauge sector: near-degenerate eigenvalues arise from a
variety of sources --- gauge orbits, accidental kinematic
degeneracies between coupled fields, and small-parameter detunings
of mass terms --- and the worst-case condition number
$\operatorname{cond}(V)$ can exceed $10^{13}$~\cite{golub2013matrix,higham2008functions} on the multi-field
theories of~\cref{Results}. At that conditioning the diagonalisation
accumulates roundoff far above the level at which backward-stable
matrix-exponential algorithms remain
accurate~\cite{higham2008functions,molervanloan2003nineteen,golub2013matrix,trefethen1997numerical}.
We adopt the recommendation of~\cite{higham2008functions}~\S~2.3: the cond-$V$ threshold above which
diagonalisation should be replaced by a backward-stable scheme. The Pad\'e
path is by contrast robust at arbitrary
$\operatorname{cond}(V)$~\cite{higham2009scaling,higham2008functions}.
The wall-time penalty for adopting it is modest: on three
representative campaign theories the ratio of Pad\'e wall time to the
former eigendecomposition wall time lies in the interval
$0.49$--$1.06$ (\cref{tab:PadeBenchmark}), so we incur no significant
performance cost in exchange for the robustness guarantee. The
eigendecomposition path remains available internally for the
closed-form Duhamel kernel used in the perturbative-reduction
scheme of~\cref{ConstraintBarrier}, which requires the
per-eigenmode expansion explicitly.

\paragraph*{Block-aware union-find} The evolution matrix $M(k)$
inherits the block structure of the underlying coupled-field
equations. A typical multi-field theory decomposes into several
decoupled sub-blocks --- for example, the $h_\times\!\leftrightarrow\!
a_y$ graviton--photon pair separates from the trace--vector torsion
channel at the linear level (see~\cref{ChannelStructure} for the
numerical demonstration on the survey theories) --- and processing
such a block-diagonal matrix as a single dense object would waste
both wall time (since the per-mode \lstinline!expm! cost scales as
$\mathcal{O}(b^3)$ in the block size $b$) and conditioning. We therefore
precompute the block decomposition: slots $i$ and $j$ are deemed
coupled when either off-diagonal entry $M_{ij}$ or $M_{ji}$ exceeds
a heuristic cut-off of $10^{-14}\,\max|M|$ in absolute value (the
kinetic matrix is not in general symmetric on multi-field theories
with non-symmetric kinetic structure), and a union-find with path
compression~\cite{tarjan1975unionfind} collects the resulting
coupling graph into connected components. The exponential is then
applied block-by-block: replacing a single block of size $b$ by $n$
independent blocks of size $b/n$ reduces the total per-mode cost from
$\mathcal{O}(b^3)$ to $\mathcal{O}(b^3/n^2)$, where the $b^3$ scaling is the standard
cost of Pad\'e scaling-and-squaring on a dense block of
size~$b$~\cite{higham2009scaling}. For the representative case of
one $12\!\times\!12$ block split into two $6\!\times\!6$ blocks this
is a factor of $4$, and we observe both the speed-up and an
unconditional improvement in numerical conditioning. The block
decomposition is retained under the Pad\'e path of the previous
paragraph for the same reason; the union-find threshold is an
engineering heuristic and is deliberately conservative.

\paragraph*{Constraint elimination via Fourier Schur complement}
When the equation system carries algebraic constraints
(\lstinline!time_order!~$=0$ fields) and is otherwise modal-eligible,
we eliminate the constraint slots per mode by Schur complement before
forming the exponential. Partitioning the per-mode state
$\hat y(k) = (y_d, y_c)^{\mathsf{T}}$ into a dynamical part $y_d$ and
a constraint part $y_c$, the equation
$\partial_t \hat y(k) = M(k)\, \hat y(k)$ of~\cref{ModalPerMode} reads
in block form
\begin{align}
  \begin{pmatrix} \partial_t y_d \\ \partial_t y_c \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} A(k) & \mathsf{S}_{dc}(k) \\
    \mathsf{S}_{cd}(k) & \mathsf{S}_{cc}(k) \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix} y_d \\ y_c \end{pmatrix}
  \, .
  \label{ModalSchur}
\end{align}
The constraint block is algebraic (no time derivative on the
constraint slots, $\partial_t y_c = 0$), so the constraint row reduces
to $\mathsf{S}_{cd}\, y_d + \mathsf{S}_{cc}\, y_c = 0$, yielding
\begin{align}
  y_c = -\mathsf{S}_{cc}^{-1}\!\mathsf{S}_{cd}\, y_d
  \, ,
  \label{SchurReducedConstraint}
\end{align}
and substitution into the dynamical block produces a reduced
evolution matrix on $y_d$ alone. For systems with a rank-deficient kinetic
matrix (degenerate kinetic eigenstructures, or gauge fields with
manifestly gauge-symmetric kinetic terms) the same reduced exponential is
evaluated through the generalised eigenvalue problem
$(K - \lambda M)\, v = 0$, which is solved internally by the QZ
algorithm of Moler \& Stewart~\cite{moler1973algorithm} as exposed by
\lstinline!scipy.linalg.eig(A, B)!. QZ handles rank-deficiency cleanly
through the Schur form without requiring the eigenvector matrix to be
well conditioned. The Schur-complement reduction itself is standard
DAE practice~\cite{brenan1996numerical,hairer1996ode2}, and a unified
entry point shared by both the modal solver and the constraint
initial-condition machinery of~\cref{ConstraintIC} ensures that the
same Schur operator is used in both contexts.

\paragraph*{Position-dependent coefficients via Krylov action}
Position-dependent coefficients --- a Gaussian magnetic-field profile
$\Bzero(x)$, a localised plasma mass term, a coordinate-dependent
gauge-fixing source --- couple the per-mode systems together. The
product $c(x)\, u(x)$ becomes a Fourier convolution,
\begin{align}
  \widehat{c \cdot u}(k)
  = (\hat c \ast \hat u)(k)
  = \int \hat c(k - k')\, \hat u(k')\, \diff k'
  \, ,
  \label{ModalConvolution}
\end{align}
and the per-mode decoupling of~\cref{ModalPerMode} fails: every mode
$\hat u(k)$ now sees a contribution from every other mode
$\hat u(k')$ through the convolution kernel. The evolution then takes
the mode-coupled form
\begin{align}
  \partial_t \hat y(k)
  = \int M(k, k')\, \hat y(k')\, \diff k'
  \, ,
  \label{ModalCoupled}
\end{align}
where $M(k, k')$ inherits both the standard $k$-multiplier structure
and the convolution kernel from $\hat c$. On the numerical grid this
collapses to a single dense matrix of dimension
$N_{\rm modes}\!\times\!N_{\rm slots}$. This matrix is generally non-normal, in which case the
eigendecomposition picture breaks down at the level of the operator,
not just at the level of finite-precision arithmetic: individual
eigenvalues $\exp(\lambda t)$ would overflow even in exact arithmetic
because $\lambda$ has a large positive real part, while the action
$\exp(M t)\, y_0$ remains bounded. This is the pseudospectral
phenomenon analysed in Trefethen \& Embree~\cite{trefethen2005spectra}
\S~15, where the transient growth of $\|\exp(A t)\|$ for non-normal
$A$ is controlled by the $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum rather than by
the spectrum itself. We
bypass the eigendecomposition entirely on this path and compute the
action of the matrix exponential directly via the Krylov scheme of
\lstinline!scipy.sparse.linalg.expm_multiply!~\cite{almohy2011action}.
For localised coefficient profiles the Fourier convolution kernel
decays exponentially in mode separation, so the convolution matrix is
effectively banded; \TIDAL{} converts it to compressed-sparse-column
(CSC) format~\cite{demmel1999supernodal,lidemmel2003superlu} when the
density falls below a heuristic thirty-percent cut-off, and the
resulting per-snapshot cost drops substantially relative to the dense
path (\cref{tab:SparseCSCBenchmark}).

\paragraph*{Nyquist mode and energy conservation} A subtlety in the
modal solver controls energy conservation at the level of machine
precision. The real-to-complex FFT's Nyquist bin (the $k=N/2$ mode on
even-$N$ grids) must be purely real for real-valued
fields~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev}, but the per-mode evolution matrix
has complex entries from the gradient couplings, so $\exp(M t)$
generates an imaginary Nyquist component which \lstinline!irfft!
silently discards on each reconstruction. We zero the Nyquist mode
in the initial condition before evolution, which is the linear
analogue of the dealiasing rule used by spectral codes for nonlinear
problems~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev,burns2020dedalus}. With the Nyquist
zero in place the relative energy drift falls to machine precision
across the example library (\cref{tab:NyquistEnergy}); without it,
the same systems exhibit a resolution-independent drift of order
$10^{-5}$~\cite{boyd2001chebyshev}. The convergence of the modal
solver against the analytic Boccaletti baseline

\subsection{Other backends}%
\label{OtherBackends}

\paragraph*{\IDA{} --- differential--algebraic equations}
The \IDA{} backend is the implicit differential--algebraic
solver from \SUNDIALS{}~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}, accessed via the
\lstinline!scikit-sundae!~\cite{randall2024skitsundae} \Python{} wrapper.
\IDA{} integrates a residual form $F(t, y, y') = 0$ by adaptive
backward differentiation formulas of orders $1$ through $5$.
\TIDAL{} populates the residual with $F_i = y_i'- R_i(y)$ for
dynamical fields (with $R_i$ the Euler--Lagrange right-hand side),
and with $F_i = R_i(y)$ for constraint fields, so that a single
Newton iteration handles both classes simultaneously. \IDA{} is
the only \TIDAL{} backend that supports algebraic constraints natively
at run time. We pair it with the analytical
Jacobian of~\cref{AnalyticalJacobian}.

\paragraph*{\CVODE{} --- adaptive ODE} The \CVODE{}
backend wraps the \SUNDIALS{} ordinary-differential-equation solver of
the same name~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials} through
\lstinline!scikit-sundae!~\cite{randall2024skitsundae}, and is
auto-selected for pure wave systems in which every equation has
time order~$2$ and a canonical Hamiltonian structure is recorded in
the specification. \CVODE{} supports both
backward-differentiation formulas (orders $1$ through $5$, suitable
for the mild stiffness that finite-difference discretisations
introduce through eigenvalues scaling as $1/\Delta
x^2$~\cite{hairer1996ode2}) and the Adams family (orders $1$ through
$12$, for non-stiff problems~\cite{hairer1993ode1}).
Constraint fields, when present in the equation specification but not
eliminable by the modal Schur path, are frozen at their
initial-condition values; systems with algebraic constraints therefore
route to \IDA{} instead.

\paragraph*{Leapfrog --- symplectic St\"ormer--Verlet and Yoshida}
The leapfrog backend implements two symplectic integrators. The first
is the second-order St\"ormer--Verlet
scheme~\cite{hairer2006geometric}, with a single force evaluation per
timestep. The second is the fourth-order Yoshida composition of three
St\"ormer--Verlet sub-steps~\cite{yoshida1990symplectic}, in which a
negative middle-step weight cancels the leading
$\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$ error of St\"ormer--Verlet. Adjacent
half-kicks between the three sub-steps merge into a single combined
kick (the Forest--Ruth fused-kick
scheme~\cite{forestruth1990fourth}), reducing the cost from six force
evaluations per outer step to three plus one initialiser. Both integrators preserve a shadow Hamiltonian to
machine precision and exhibit zero secular energy drift over arbitrary
integration times~\cite{hairer2006geometric}. We retain the leapfrog
backend as an opt-in scheme (\lstinline!--scheme leapfrog!) for
long-time conservative runs; the dispatcher never selects it
automatically, because the adaptive solvers are a safer default on
systems whose stability properties are not yet understood.

\paragraph*{\SciPy{} --- general-purpose adaptive ODE} The \SciPy{}
backend wraps \lstinline!scipy.integrate.solve_ivp!~\cite{virtanen2020scipy},
with a default method of DOP853 (an eighth-order embedded
Runge--Kutta scheme~\cite{dormand1980runge,hairer1993ode1}) and the
Radau and BDF families available through \lstinline!--method!. We retain
this backend for two reasons. First, smooth non-stiff problems are
sometimes solved more cheaply by an explicit high-order method than by
adaptive BDF, because DOP853 requires fewer function evaluations per
unit of accuracy. Second, an independent \SciPy{} integration path
provides a cross-check on the \SUNDIALS{} implementations; the per-theory
agreement numbers are reported in~\cref{Validation}. Like leapfrog,
the \SciPy{} backend is opt-in only.

\subsection{Initial-condition preparation}%
\label{ICPreparation}

\paragraph*{Plane-wave projection onto the discrete Fourier basis} The
plane-wave initial condition
$\psi(x_i, 0) = A\cos(k_0 x_i)$ is a sampled trigonometric function on
the $N$ grid points of a periodic interval $[0, L]$. The discrete
Fourier modes available on this grid are $k_n = 2\pi n / L$ for
$n = 0, 1, \ldots, N/2$, and the sampled field is monochromatic in the
discrete representation only when $k_0$ coincides with some $k_n$.
For any other $k_0$ the discrete Fourier transform of the sampled
values has non-zero amplitude on every mode, falling off as
$|k_n - k_0|^{-1}$: this is spectral leakage of an off-grid wavevector
onto the on-grid basis. In linearly stable systems the leakage is
benign --- each contaminated mode oscillates at its own frequency and
contributes only a small decoration to observables --- but in systems
with tachyonic eigenvalues at some $k$-modes the leakage onto those
modes is exponentially amplified over the simulation interval, and the
solver's perturbation-validity guard rejects the run even though the
intended physical IC would have been stable. The plane-wave constructor
\lstinline!_plane_wave_slots! therefore snaps each component of the
requested wavevector to the nearest discrete mode on every periodic axis,
clamped one below Nyquist; a user-visible \texttt{Note} is emitted
whenever the snap changes $k$ by more than one percent. The override
\lstinline!--ic-no-snap! recovers the legacy verbatim-$k$ behaviour for
reproducibility of pre-snap runs. The snap is critical to the PGT
amplification surveys of~\cref{Results}: without it, $k_0$ values
chosen for physics reasons leak amplitude onto the redundant
TorsionCDT state directions and produce spurious divergences within
the linearized regime.

\subsection{Constraint initialisation}%
\label{ConstraintIC}

\paragraph*{Motivation} When the equation specification contains
algebraic constraints, the user-supplied initial condition
$(q_0, v_0)$ generically lies off the constraint surface. The Maxwell
$A_0$ satisfies an instantaneous Helmholtz equation
$(\nabla^2 - m^2)\, A_0 = \mathcal{S}[A_i]$ in the Lorenz gauge, the
linearised
gravitational-wave metric satisfies a transversality condition, and
similar constraints arise from every gauge-fixed sector of the
symbolic stage (see~\cref{Architecture}). Without projection of
$(q_0, v_0)$ onto the constraint surface --- \ie{} without solving the
algebraic equations of motion for the constraint slots given the
user-supplied dynamical-slot values --- the \IDA{} Newton
iteration fails to converge at the first timestep, and the modal Schur
path produces an inconsistent reduced state. The
\lstinline!ensure_consistent_ic! function performs this projection
once, after the initial condition is assembled and before the chosen backend is invoked.

\paragraph*{Three-tier solver} The projection is implemented as a
three-tier solver. The first tier handles the common case in which
all boundaries are periodic and the constraint coefficients are
constant: the constraint operator is diagonalised by the discrete
Fourier transform, with FD-order-aware modified wavenumbers
$k_{\rm mod}^2 = -(2/\Delta x^2)(1 - \cos(k\,\Delta x))$ at order~$2$
and the analogous higher-order expressions at orders~$4$ and~$6$ so
that the constraint solution is spectrally consistent with the FD
stencils used during evolution; the cost is
$\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$~\cite{cooley1965fft}. The second
tier covers non-periodic boundaries and variable coefficients: the
constraint Jacobian is built by impulse-probing the same
\lstinline!RHSEvaluator! operators used during evolution, then stored as
CSC and factored by SuperLU~\cite{demmel1999supernodal,lidemmel2003superlu} with
$\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ build cost and $\mathcal{O}(N)$ solve. The third tier, reserved for
the $N>2\times 10^{5}$ regime, is a Krylov iterative scheme; it is
implemented but not exercised in the campaigns of~\cref{Results},
where the largest grids fit comfortably within tier~$2$. The
dispatcher \lstinline!_select_method! chooses the fastest applicable
tier from the boundary conditions and coefficient structure recorded
in the specification.

\paragraph*{Zero-mode regularisation} A pure Poisson constraint on a
periodic domain has a one-dimensional null space: the zero Fourier
mode is undetermined by the elliptic problem and must be supplied by
an external condition. We pin the zero mode to zero as a numerical
regularisation. For the gauge-field constraints relevant to the
present work --- the Maxwell scalar potential $A_0$ and similar
four-potentials --- physical observables are constructed from
derivatives (the field strengths $F_{\mu\nu}$), and the zero-mode
choice cancels in any gauge-invariant quantity. The same is not
automatic for non-gauge constraint fields, where the field value itself
may carry physical content; such cases are handled instead by
source-side normalisation in the spec. This is a numerical gauge fix,
distinct from the algebraic gauge-fixing presets implemented at the
symbolic stage and discussed in~\cref{GaugeFixing}.

\subsection{Analytical Jacobian}%
\label{AnalyticalJacobian}

\paragraph*{Motivation} The implicit backends \IDA{} and
\CVODE{} both rely on a Jacobian matrix $J = \partial F/\partial y +
c_j\,\partial F/\partial y'$ to drive the Newton iteration of each
backward-differentiation step. Finite-difference approximation of
this matrix requires multiple residual evaluations per Newton
iteration~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}.\footnote{Specifically, the
cost is $\mathcal{O}(n_{\rm colors})$ residual evaluations per Newton
iteration, where $n_{\rm colors}$ is the chromatic number of the
sparsity graph~\cite{curtispowellreid1974}: each column-colour group
of structurally non-overlapping entries can be probed in a single
residual evaluation.} For the multi-field systems with
$N\!\sim\!10^4$ degrees of freedom that arise in the PGT campaigns
of~\cref{Results}, that overhead is the dominant cost. We circumvent
it by precomputing the Jacobian analytically, once, from the symbolic
right-hand-side terms in the equation specification, and supplying
it to the linear solver directly through the \SUNDIALS{} callback
interface of~\cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}.

\paragraph*{Three-tier dispatch} The analytical-Jacobian machinery
maintains three distinct delivery tiers, with thresholds $N \leq
2{,}000$ for the dense tier, $2{,}000<N\leq 200{,}000$ for the sparse
tier, and $N>200{,}000$ for the matrix-free tier. The dense tier
fills a two-dimensional \NumPy{} array with $\partial F/\partial y +
c_j\,\partial F/\partial y'$ at every Newton step, which the \SUNDIALS{}
direct linear solver factors by LAPACK LU; this path delivers a
five-fold wall-time speedup over finite differencing on
representative small systems. The sparse tier instead fills a flat
CSC data buffer paired with a fixed sparsity
pattern, which \SUNDIALS{} factors with
SuperLU~\cite{demmel1999supernodal,lidemmel2003superlu}; this yields
a measurable speedup for both backends, larger for \IDA{}
(heavier per-step Jacobian cost) than for \CVODE{}. The
matrix-free tier supplies an analytical
Jacobian--vector product callback $J\,v = (\partial F/\partial y)\,v +
c_j\,(\partial F/\partial y')\,v$ to the \SUNDIALS{} Krylov interface,
which then solves the linear step by GMRES~\cite{saad1986gmres}
without ever materialising $J$ explicitly --- this is the regime in
which the $\mathcal{O}(n_{\rm colors})$ overhead of finite differencing would
dominate absolutely.

\paragraph*{Coordinate accumulation} The sparse Jacobian matrix is
built by accumulating contributions from each operator into a
coordinate (COO) triplet buffer~\cite{davis2006sparse}, then
converting once at the end to compressed-sparse-column format. We
adopted this scheme after the profiler identified the naive
list-of-lists assembly as the bottleneck: at a $48\!\times\!48$ grid
the LIL-based assembly took $1{,}700\,\mathrm{ms}$ per Jacobian, while
the COO path takes $4\,\mathrm{ms}$, a more than four-hundred-fold
improvement that brought Jacobian construction down from the dominant
cost to a negligible fraction of the per-step budget. The cost scales
linearly with the number of non-zero entries rather than with the
system size.

\paragraph*{Circulant operators on periodic grids} A further saving
is available when the boundary conditions are all periodic and the
coefficients are spatially constant: the spatial-operator matrices
are then circulant, since each row is a cyclic shift of the same
stencil. We exploit this by probing a single grid point and tiling
the resulting stencil row across all $N$ rows via modular arithmetic,
an $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{nnz})$ construction in place of the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ assembly
of the general sparse path. On the periodic-domain Einstein--Maxwell
system this contributes an additional factor of roughly $28\times$
in operator-matrix construction.

\subsection{Dispatch and runtime workflow}%
\label{Dispatch}

The JSON equation specification produced by the symbolic stage
of~\cref{SymbolicComputation} and consumed by the solvers above
is illustrated in~\cref{JSONExample}: it records the dynamical
fields (tensor type and time-derivative order), the right-hand-side
triples of each equation of motion (each triple carries a resolved
numerical coefficient, an operator name from the catalogue
of~\cref{SpatialDiscretisation}, and a target field name, summed
to form the Euler--Lagrange right-hand side), the Hamiltonian terms
used by the energy diagnostic, and the mass and coupling matrices
used by the modal solver.

\paragraph*{Result handling} Every backend returns a uniform result
dictionary --- snapshot times, the corresponding flat state vectors,
a success boolean, and a human-readable status string --- which is
serialised to the output directory together with the resolved
equation specification and the runtime parameter values. Downstream
measurement and analysis subcommands consume this directory directly,
so a run can be re-analysed without re-reading the original
\xAct{} session. The two-stage architecture diagram
of~\cref{Architecture} summarises the full data flow.
